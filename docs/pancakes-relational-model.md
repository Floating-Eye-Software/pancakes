# Abstract

People do not act in isolation.

What a person, household, institution, object, practice, or place can do and become depends partly upon the conditions and connections within which it exists. Some of those connections are momentary. Others become historically structured: what happened before alters the present conditions under which later interaction occurs.

This document defines the relational grammar used throughout Pancakes.

It distinguishes:

* context from the context selected for a particular inquiry,
* relation from relationship,
* bidirectional consequence from reciprocal agency,
* history from memory,
* reality from recognition and representation,
* relational involvement from responsibility,
* and place from a person's relationship with a place.

The central principle is:

> **When prior interaction alters present conditions in a way that remains consequential for what can happen next, history has acquired structural force in the present.**

Pancakes calls this **historical carryover**.

Historical carryover appears in friendships, homes, books, artifacts, practices, ecosystems, institutions, and places. These are not all the same kind of thing. Relationality provides a common grammar through which their similarities and differences can be described without reducing everything important to one category.

---

# 1. Purpose

Pancakes already depends upon relational concepts.

It describes:

* people participating in institutions,
* households depending upon infrastructure,
* communities caring for shared resources,
* stewards assuming responsibility without ownership,
* places accumulating the consequences of transformations,
* artifacts preserving provenance,
* institutions carrying commitments through succession,
* and symbolic projections interpreting shared events in different ways.

These uses require a stable vocabulary.

Without one, several different claims can collapse into the same language:

* saying that something is physically affected,
* saying that an action has become possible,
* saying that an event has acquired meaning,
* saying that a person has accepted an obligation,
* saying that an institution recognizes a status,
* and saying that a history continues to shape future participation.

The relational model separates these claims while explaining how they may interact.

It is not a claim that everything is meaningfully related to everything else. It is a method for identifying consequential connections, explaining how they operate, and determining when their histories have become structurally important.

---

# 2. The Relational Grammar

The model uses the following progression:

```text
operative context
    ↓
relation
    ↓
interaction or transformation
    ↓
altered present conditions
    ↓
historical carryover
    ↓
path-dependent future interaction
```

This is not a required life cycle.

A context may contain relations that never produce an observed interaction.

An interaction may be transient and leave no consequential change.

A single interaction may immediately alter later possibilities.

A long-running relation may remain comparatively unchanged.

The progression instead identifies the point at which history becomes part of the present organization of a relation.

The model's core concepts are:

| Concept | Canonical meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Context | The surrounding organization of conditions through which present possibilities and consequences are shaped. |
| Relevant context | The portion of operative context that matters to a particular activity, decision, explanation, or representation. |
| Relation | An identifiable connection through which one term can make a consequential difference to another. |
| Interaction | An occurrence in which the terms of a relation affect one another or participate in a common process. |
| Historical carryover | The continuing consequence of prior interaction because it altered present conditions. |
| Relationship | A relation whose prior course has become one of the present conditions governing its future course. |
| Agentic relationship | A relationship in which more than one party can independently interpret, initiate, answer, refuse, or contest. |
| Relational configuration | An organized set of overlapping relations that together conditions participation or change. |
| Place | A materially situated relational configuration that conditions participation within a locality. |

These definitions are elaborated below.

# 3. Context

## 3.1 Operative context

**Context is the surrounding organization of conditions through which an entity's present possibilities or consequences are shaped.**

Context may include:

* physical conditions,
* ecological processes,
* available resources,
* infrastructure,
* other people,
* institutions,
* laws and conventions,
* histories,
* expectations,
* records,
* practices,
* and current states of knowledge.

Context exists before any one observer describes it completely.

A household depends upon a watershed even when nobody in the household is thinking about hydrology.

A building's exits affect safety even when they are absent from the current floor plan.

An institution's precedent may shape a decision even when a participant has not been told that the precedent exists.

Context is therefore not identical to whatever information a person or system happens to possess.

## 3.2 Relevant context

No activity can represent all operative conditions.

**Relevant context is the portion of operative context that bears upon a particular activity, decision, explanation, or model.**

The relevant context for repairing a bicycle differs from the relevant context for insuring it, lending it, interpreting its symbolic provenance, or determining whether its manufacture involved exploitation.

Relevance is purpose-dependent.

The underlying context is not.

This distinction allows Pancakes to remain selective without pretending that unrepresented conditions do not exist.

## 3.3 Represented context

A person, institution, or information system may recognize and record part of the relevant context.

That representation may be:

* incomplete,
* uncertain,
* private,
* disputed,
* out of date,
* purpose-specific,
* or incorrect.

A Pancakes node therefore maintains representations and claims about context. It does not contain the context itself.

This epistemic boundary is essential to privacy and institutional humility.

# 4. Relation

## 4.1 Canonical definition

**A relation is an identifiable connection through which the state, action, standing, or history of one term can make a consequential difference to the possibilities, state, interpretation, or standing of another.**

The terms of a relation may include:

* persons,
* groups,
* households,
* communities,
* institutions,
* objects,
* artifacts,
* practices,
* services,
* resources,
* processes,
* ecosystems,
* or places.

Calling something a term of a relation does not imply that it is an agent, has experiences, acts voluntarily, or possesses rights.

## 4.2 Consequence and mechanism

A useful relational claim identifies both a consequence and a pathway.

Examples include:

* a watershed affects a household through water, drainage, flooding, and infrastructure;
* an employer affects a worker through wages, rules, schedules, authority, and dependency;
* a book affects a reader through language, interpretation, memory, and later action;
* a reader affects a book through annotation, preservation, circulation, and interpretation;
* a public-health institution affects residents through information, services, rules, and emergency powers.

The connection may be:

* physical,
* ecological,
* practical,
* informational,
* interpretive,
* social,
* legal,
* economic,
* institutional,
* or symbolic.

Several mechanisms may operate at once.

## 4.3 Why this does not mean everything is related

Everything may have remote causal ancestry or indirect physical influence.

Pancakes does not treat every such connection as an active relation within every model.

A relational claim should specify:

1. the terms being connected;
2. the domain and scale of the claim;
3. the pathway through which consequence occurs;
4. what possibilities, conditions, interpretations, or standings are affected;
5. and whether the connection is current, historical, potential, or claimed.

An unspecified assertion that everything is connected explains nothing.

A bounded account of how water, authority, expectation, access, or interpretation connects particular terms can explain a great deal.

## 4.4 Relations may be directional

A relation need not affect its terms in the same way.

A person may depend upon an electrical grid more than the grid depends upon that person.

An institution may exercise authority over someone who possesses little practical influence over the institution.

A book and a reader may both be changed through reading, but only the reader interprets and responds as an agent.

Direction, dependence, power, and agency must therefore be represented separately.

# 5. Interaction and Transformation

## 5.1 Interaction

**An interaction is an occurrence in which the terms of a relation affect one another or participate in a common process.**

Interactions may be:

* deliberate or incidental,
* cooperative or adversarial,
* direct or mediated,
* recognized or unrecognized,
* voluntary or compulsory,
* material or interpretive,
* transient or consequential.

Not every interaction becomes historically important.

## 5.2 Transformation

**A transformation is an occurrence that changes the state, standing, capability, organization, or possible continuation of one or more terms.**

Examples include:

* repairing a bicycle,
* annotating a book,
* learning a skill,
* restoring a wetland,
* signing a contract,
* reorganizing an institution,
* changing a road,
* ending a membership,
* or establishing a recurring practice.

A transformation may change:

* material form,
* ecological conditions,
* available action,
* capability,
* knowledge,
* legal standing,
* institutional procedure,
* expectation,
* interpretation,
* or symbolic meaning.

These changes should not be collapsed into one undifferentiated idea of meaning.

## 5.3 Interaction does not guarantee historical carryover

An interaction becomes historically consequential only when it alters present conditions in a way that matters to later continuation.

A person may pass through a railway station once without changing either the station or their future use of it.

Another encounter at the same station may establish a routine, cause an injury, produce a friendship, reveal an inaccessible route, or create an association that changes every later visit.

The event alone does not determine whether a relationship has formed.

Its continuing consequences do.

---

# 6. Historical Carryover

## 6.1 Canonical definition

**Historical carryover occurs when prior interaction has altered present conditions in a way that remains consequential for future interaction or participation.**

The earlier event need not remain present as an object.

What matters is that the present is now organized differently because the event occurred.

Examples include:

* money was lent and an obligation now exists;
* a bridge was destroyed and a route is no longer available;
* a forest burned and ecological succession now proceeds from changed conditions;
* a person learned to ride a bicycle and now possesses a capability;
* two parcels were legally merged and now have different standing;
* a promise altered what the parties may reasonably expect from one another;
* a workshop developed practices that shape how later work is taught and performed.

## 6.2 Carriers of historical carryover

Historical carryover often persists through identifiable carriers, including:

* physical alteration,
* ecological state,
* bodily capacity or injury,
* memory,
* habit,
* expectation,
* skill,
* artifact,
* record,
* precedent,
* role,
* convention,
* reputation,
* legal status,
* infrastructure,
* or institutional procedure.

These carriers are mechanisms through which historical carryover persists.

They are not a substance called history.

## 6.3 History, state, record, and memory

Pancakes distinguishes:

* **history:** what occurred;
* **present state:** the conditions that now exist partly because of what occurred;
* **record:** an intentionally preserved representation or claim about what occurred;
* **memory:** an agent's or system's capacity to retain, reconstruct, interpret, or use aspects of the past;
* **historical carryover:** the continuing effect of the past upon present and future possibilities.

These are related but not interchangeable.

A place may retain physical consequences that nobody remembers.

A record may survive after its practical consequence has ended.

A person may remember an event inaccurately while still acting according to that memory.

An institution may preserve a decision in its archive while failing to preserve the capability needed to act upon it.

## 6.4 Historical carryover without literal memory

Books, tools, buildings, landscapes, and relationships do not need to be imagined as little minds or containers of memory.

A book may retain annotations.

A tool may retain wear and modification.

A building may retain renovations and damage.

A landscape may retain altered drainage, vegetation, paths, or contamination.

A relationship may continue through expectations, habits, obligations, records, and changed participants.

The canonical claim is not that these things literally remember.

It is that their histories remain active in present conditions.

# 7. Relationship

## 7.1 Canonical definition

**A relationship is a relation whose prior course has become one of the present conditions governing its future course.**

This definition makes relationship a historically structured or path-dependent relation.

A chance encounter may establish a relation without yet establishing a relationship.

A single consequential encounter may nevertheless create a relationship immediately if it changes later expectations, standing, access, obligation, or conduct.

Duration and repetition often deepen relationships.

They do not define them by themselves.

## 7.2 Relationships with non-agents

A person may have a relationship with:

* a book,
* a home,
* a tool,
* a craft,
* a recurring activity,
* an institution,
* a landscape,
* or a place.

These uses are legitimate when the prior course of the relation affects later interaction.

A familiar path is encountered differently because it has been walked before.

An annotated book is read differently because of earlier reading.

A long-used tool participates in later work through wear, adaptation, familiarity, and skill.

The non-agentic term does not need to interpret the relationship for the relationship to be historically structured.

## 7.3 Agentic relationships

**An agentic relationship is a relationship in which more than one party can independently interpret, initiate, answer, refuse, or contest.**

Agentic relationships include many:

* friendships,
* family relationships,
* partnerships,
* mentorships,
* care relationships,
* professional relationships,
* political relationships,
* and relationships between people and institutions exercising organized agency.

Agency introduces questions that do not arise in the same form for books, tools, or buildings:

* consent,
* voice,
* answerability,
* recognition,
* refusal,
* interpretation,
* dignity,
* repair,
* and exit.

## 7.4 Bidirectional consequence is not reciprocal agency

Effects may run in both directions without the terms possessing the same capacities.

A reader may annotate, preserve, lend, damage, or reinterpret a book.

The book may reorganize the reader's understanding and later behaviour.

The consequences are bidirectional.

The agency is not.

Similarly, an ecosystem may respond dynamically to human intervention without becoming one unified party capable of consent or institutional answer.

Pancakes must not import interpersonal assumptions into every historically structured relation.

## 7.5 Relationships need not be equal, voluntary, or good

A relationship may be:

* chosen or inherited,
* recognized or denied,
* supportive or harmful,
* equal or asymmetric,
* reciprocal or extractive,
* revisable or difficult to escape,
* legitimate or illegitimate.

The existence of a relationship proves none of these qualities.

Continued participation proves neither acceptance nor consent.

Historical depth may strengthen care and understanding.

It may also strengthen dependency, domination, lock-in, trauma, or exclusion.

# 8. Relational Configurations

## 8.1 More than one relation

Many important Pancakes structures cannot be reduced to a single connection between two terms.

A household includes relationships among people, rooms, routines, resources, obligations, technologies, institutions, and surrounding places.

A school includes buildings, students, workers, families, curricula, law, infrastructure, authority, records, practices, and communities.

A watershed includes water, terrain, organisms, infrastructure, households, industries, institutions, and downstream effects.

These are relational configurations.

## 8.2 Canonical definition

**A relational configuration is an organized set of overlapping relations that together conditions participation, interpretation, or change.**

A configuration may be:

* temporary or durable,
* materially situated or distributed,
* formally governed or emergent,
* recognized or only partly understood,
* stable or undergoing transformation.

History is not required for a configuration to exist.

Historical carryover may nevertheless make one configuration develop differently from another that initially appeared similar.

## 8.3 Configuration is not totality

No model captures every relation operating within a configuration.

The boundary of a configuration depends partly upon the process being described.

A school may be described as:

* a building,
* a catchment,
* an educational institution,
* a workplace,
* a community,
* or part of a wider constitutional system.

These are overlapping accounts of operative relations, not competing claims that only one school exists.

# 9. Place as a Relational Configuration

The Pancakes Place Model provides the full theory of place.

For the purposes of the relational model:

> **A place is a materially situated configuration of overlapping relations that conditions participation within a locality.**

Place is therefore not a special kind of relationship.

A person, household, institution, or community may have a relationship with a place.

The place itself contains and participates in many relations that exceed any one participant's relationship with it.

A house may simultaneously participate in:

* an inhabitant's relationship with home,
* another person's relationship with a childhood home,
* landlord and tenant relations,
* property law,
* utility dependencies,
* neighbourhood life,
* drainage and weather,
* ecological processes,
* architectural history,
* and the material effects of previous occupants.

No one of these is the whole place.

Place is distinctive because it is materially situated. Physical structure, ecology, infrastructure, proximity, movement, and shared exposure can affect participants whether or not they interpret the place in the same way.

A place may be temporary.

Examples include:

* a campsite,
* a festival ground,
* a protest encampment,
* an emergency shelter,
* or a classroom assembled for an afternoon.

Durable places can accumulate extensive historical carryover, but continuity is not a requirement of place itself.

The revised Pancakes Place Model should distinguish:

* the operative relations composing a place,
* relationships that participants develop with it,
* histories that occurred there,
* present conditions altered by those histories,
* meanings attributed by different participants,
* and symbolic representations of place history.

---

# 10. Institutions as Relational Configurations

An institution is not merely an organization or legal person.

Within Pancakes, an institution is a persistent system of:

* expectations,
* roles,
* responsibilities,
* practices,
* rules,
* records,
* resources,
* authority,
* and relationships

that coordinates participation across time.

An institution is therefore a governed relational configuration.

It is not one relationship.

People may have relationships with the institution, its representatives, its purposes, its services, and one another.

Institutional history may persist through:

* precedent,
* procedure,
* trained participants,
* infrastructure,
* records,
* commitments,
* reputation,
* unresolved conflict,
* and distributions of capability and power.

Institutional recognition may also constitute relations such as:

* membership,
* office,
* employment,
* license,
* contract,
* jurisdiction,
* and delegated authority.

The existence of institutional records does not make an institution's interpretation complete or unchallengeable.

# 11. Reality, Recognition, and Representation

## 11.1 Recognition-independent relations

Some relations operate whether or not anyone recognizes them.

Examples include:

* water flowing through a watershed,
* a household's exposure to flood risk,
* pollution affecting an ecosystem,
* a building depending upon a power grid,
* or a road connecting a neighbourhood to essential services.

Discovery changes knowledge.

It does not create the material relation.

## 11.2 Recognized relations

A person, community, institution, or system may recognize a relation that already operates.

Recognition may:

* make the relation legible,
* change behaviour,
* support coordination,
* create new responsibilities,
* produce disagreement,
* or motivate transformation.

Recognition can therefore alter a relation without having created its original mechanism.

## 11.3 Recognition-constituted relations

Some relations exist partly through appropriate recognition, agreement, convention, or record.

Examples include:

* contracts,
* memberships,
* offices,
* licenses,
* promises,
* marriages,
* and institutional delegations.

These relations are real.

Their operative mechanisms include shared interpretation, social practice, law, records, and institutional enforcement.

Recognition may be contested, invalid, fraudulent, unequal, or revoked. The existence of a record does not settle its legitimacy.

## 11.4 Representation

A representation is an account of a relation, context, history, or present state.

Representations include:

* maps,
* contracts,
* databases,
* diagrams,
* narratives,
* symbolic spectra,
* institutional records,
* and personal memories.

Representations may affect reality when people act through them.

A map may redirect movement.

A contract may establish obligations.

A false record may produce real harm.

A symbolic projection may change how someone understands a place or activity.

When a representation produces new consequences, that use becomes another interaction within the relational system.

## 11.5 Consequences for Pancakes nodes

A node decides which relations it recognizes, records, displays, or uses within its authority and permissions.

It does not decide which recognition-independent relations exist.

Nodes should therefore:

* distinguish observation from inference,
* preserve provenance,
* permit correction and dispute,
* avoid claiming complete context,
* maintain privacy boundaries,
* and allow users to remove representations where removal is legitimate even when the underlying external relation continues.

Completeness is not the goal.

Useful, proportionate, revisable understanding is.

# 12. Dimensions of Relation

Pancakes does not require a large taxonomy of mutually exclusive relation types.

Relations can instead be described along a small set of dimensions.

## 12.1 Mode of consequence

What does the relation change?

Possible modes include:

* material conditions,
* ecological conditions,
* available action,
* capability,
* interpretation,
* expectation,
* social standing,
* permission,
* obligation,
* authority,
* or symbolic meaning.

One relation may operate through several modes.

## 12.2 Historical structure

How does prior interaction affect the present relation?

A relation may be:

* newly encountered,
* episodic,
* recurring,
* historically structured,
* deeply path-dependent,
* interrupted,
* repaired,
* or ended while leaving continuing consequences.

## 12.3 Agency

Which terms can:

* perceive,
* interpret,
* initiate,
* answer,
* refuse,
* contest,
* negotiate,
* or revise their participation?

Agency may be individual, collective, institutional, partial, impaired, delegated, or disputed.

## 12.4 Direction and asymmetry

Who affects whom, and how?

Relevant questions include:

* Are consequences one-way or bidirectional?
* Who depends upon whom?
* Who controls resources or information?
* Who can alter the relation?
* Who receives benefit?
* Who bears risk?
* Who is vulnerable to withdrawal, neglect, or coercion?

Bidirectionality does not imply symmetry.

Reciprocity does not require identical contributions.

## 12.5 Recognition and constitution

Is the relation:

* unrecognized,
* observed,
* inferred,
* acknowledged,
* disputed,
* formally recorded,
* or partly constituted through recognition?

Different participants may recognize the same relation differently.

## 12.6 Entry, exit, and reversibility

How did participation begin, and how can it change?

Relevant questions include:

* Was entry chosen, inherited, incidental, or compulsory?
* Can participation be reduced or revised?
* Can consent be withdrawn?
* What dependencies survive exit?
* Which consequences can be repaired or reversed?
* Which changes are effectively irreversible?

Directness, mediation, locality, distribution, and scale should also be stated where relevant. They describe the pathway and scope of a relation rather than adding new ontological categories.

# 13. Relation, Responsibility, and Stewardship

The existence of a relation does not automatically establish responsibility.

Causal involvement alone is insufficient.

A person may be affected by a distant process without having the knowledge, capacity, authority, or practical opportunity to change it.

Responsibility may instead arise through combinations of:

* causal contribution,
* control,
* capability,
* accepted commitment,
* institutional role,
* delegated authority,
* benefit,
* dependence,
* reciprocity,
* vulnerability,
* repair of harm,
* or requirements of justice.

The relational model identifies where responsibility questions arise.

The Pancakes Power and Responsibility and Stewardship models determine how those responsibilities should be understood and allocated.

Stewardship is one possible response to a consequential relation.

It involves responsible care for people, purposes, resources, institutions, relationships, or places without reducing care to ownership or domination.

A relationship may support stewardship.

It may also be extractive, neglected, or harmful.

Historical depth does not legitimate power.

Authority must still be justified, bounded, accountable, and open to correction.

# 14. Relationship to Pitchfork

The relational model describes concepts that exist prior to any one information system.

Pitchfork provides operational and symbolic representations of selected parts of this reality.

| Relational concept | Possible Pitchfork expression |
| --- | --- |
| Interaction or transformation | Event or settled transformation |
| Changed present conditions | Updated state, inventory, capability, obligation, or contract |
| Historical carryover | Continuing state plus selected event and provenance history |
| Symbolic historical carryover | Acquired frequencies and changed symbolic spectra |
| Lineage | Preserved relationships among transformations and their inputs |
| Relationship | A historically structured relation represented through relevant state, events, commitments, and symbolic meaning |
| Place context | Place references, public facts, local relationships, and place spectra |
| Terroir | The characteristic contribution of a place's present conditions and acquired history to later transformations |
| Projection | A purpose-specific interpretation of recorded symbolic state |

## 14.1 Symbolic representation is not the whole relation

Frequencies and spectra describe symbolic meaning.

They do not establish every physical, legal, ecological, emotional, or institutional fact about a relation.

A destroyed bridge may change mobility before that consequence receives any symbolic frequency.

An outstanding debt may alter legal and practical standing through a contract and ledger state.

A learned skill may change capability whether or not a symbolic projection displays learning.

Acquired frequencies are therefore symbolic expressions of selected historical carryover, not exhaustive histories of the entities or relations to which they apply.

## 14.2 Provenance and lineage

Provenance describes where a recorded result came from.

Lineage preserves selected relationships among transformations, inputs, participants, places, services, and artifacts.

They support historical continuity without claiming that the record contains everything consequential about the past.

Privacy-preserving attenuation, filtering, and abstraction may intentionally omit parts of lineage.

Omission from a projection does not erase the underlying history.

## 14.3 Transformation and place

Place participates in symbolic transformation in two distinguishable directions.

First, a place may condition a transformation.

Its material conditions, practices, histories, and symbolic spectrum may affect what the transformation produces or means.

Second, a transformation may alter the place.

When the transformation changes present conditions and those changes remain consequential, the place has acquired historical carryover. Pitchfork may represent symbolically meaningful aspects of that carryover as acquired place frequencies or changed place spectrum.

The same event may do both.

Not every event occurring at a location changes the place's subsequent identity.

## 14.4 No duplicate relationship-memory subsystem

Pitchfork does not require a universal object called relationship memory.

Historical carryover may already be expressed through:

* current state,
* events,
* contracts,
* obligations,
* capabilities,
* provenance,
* lineage,
* acquired frequencies,
* and projections.

Implementations should preserve the distinctions among these mechanisms rather than forcing all history into one symbolic container.

# 15. Worked Examples

## 15.1 Person and person

Two people encounter one another.

The first encounter establishes a relation if each becomes consequential to the other's actions or possibilities.

The relation becomes a relationship when its prior course affects later interaction through such conditions as:

* recognition,
* expectation,
* trust,
* affection,
* obligation,
* habit,
* conflict,
* shared practice,
* or memory.

This is an agentic relationship because both people can independently interpret, answer, initiate, refuse, and contest.

It may still be asymmetric in power, dependence, knowledge, capacity, or vulnerability.

Neither participant owns the complete meaning of the relationship.

## 15.2 Person and book

A book affects a person through language, interpretation, knowledge, imagination, and later behaviour.

The person may affect the book through:

* annotation,
* preservation,
* wear,
* lending,
* translation,
* teaching,
* or public interpretation.

The relation becomes historically structured when earlier reading alters later encounters with this book, its ideas, or this particular copy.

Consequences can run in both directions.

Agency does not.

The book cannot consent to annotation or contest an interpretation.

Duties concerning the book arise through ownership, stewardship, cultural importance, commitments to other people, or institutional responsibility rather than interpersonal reciprocity with the book itself.

## 15.3 Person and home

A home conditions shelter, privacy, movement, routine, hospitality, work, access, cost, and belonging.

The person changes it through:

* arrangement,
* maintenance,
* renovation,
* decoration,
* wear,
* neglect,
* and recurring practice.

Historical carryover may persist through physical alteration, embodied routine, memory, legal standing, relationships with other occupants, and expectations about future use.

The relationship with home is not identical to the building.

The place also participates in utility, neighbourhood, property, ecological, legal, and institutional relations that exceed the inhabitant's personal experience.

## 15.4 Person and landscape

A landscape conditions movement, weather exposure, access, risk, sustenance, observation, and interpretation.

A person may alter it through travel, construction, extraction, restoration, naming, documentation, or care.

The landscape is not one unified agent.

Its physical and ecological processes may nevertheless respond dynamically to human action.

Historical carryover may persist through trails, erosion, habitat change, infrastructure, contamination, recurring practices, knowledge, and stories.

Different participants may inhabit the same landscape through different histories, capabilities, and meanings.

No one relationship exhausts the place.

## 15.5 Household and watershed

A household participates in a watershed through:

* drinking water,
* drainage,
* runoff,
* sewage,
* flood risk,
* infrastructure,
* regulation,
* and ecological effects.

These relations operate even when the household and its Pancakes node do not recognize them.

Recognition may later support preparedness, conservation, public participation, or stewardship.

It does not create the hydrological relation.

Responsibility must be allocated through contribution, capacity, control, benefit, vulnerability, institutional role, and justice rather than inferred from involvement alone.

## 15.6 Person and institution

A person may relate to an institution through:

* membership,
* employment,
* citizenship,
* enrolment,
* contract,
* service use,
* dependency,
* regulation,
* or exposure to institutional decisions.

Some of these relations are constituted through recognition and record.

Others affect the person before the person understands which institution is acting.

The relationship becomes historically structured through policies, records, precedent, reputation, fulfilled or broken commitments, accumulated benefits or harms, habits of participation, and changing power.

The institution exercises organized agency through roles, procedures, and people.

Its records do not prove consent, acceptance, or legitimacy.

Humane institutional relationships require intelligibility, voice, answerability, revision, appeal, remedy, and practical exit appropriate to the participant's dependence and exposure.

# 16. Design Implications for Pancakes

## 16.1 Model relations without claiming total context

Systems should identify the scope, mechanism, direction, and uncertainty of represented relations.

They should not imply that a graph or record exhausts reality.

## 16.2 Preserve the reality-recognition distinction

Discovery may reveal an existing relation.

Agreement or institutional recognition may constitute another.

The system should record which kind of claim is being made.

## 16.3 Keep agency explicit

Bidirectional consequences must not be mistaken for reciprocal agency.

Consent, voice, dignity, and answerability apply where relevant agents and governed participants are involved.

## 16.4 Treat history as altered present conditions

Systems should not require every historically consequential relation to possess one memory object.

Current state, capability, obligation, material alteration, practice, record, and provenance may each carry different aspects of history.

## 16.5 Do not infer legitimacy from persistence

A long relationship may be caring and resilient.

It may also be coercive, extractive, inaccessible, or impossible to leave.

Duration does not establish consent or legitimacy.

## 16.6 Do not infer responsibility from connection alone

Responsibility requires further analysis of power, contribution, capability, role, benefit, commitment, vulnerability, and justice.

## 16.7 Preserve privacy

Pancakes should help people understand consequential relations without constructing exhaustive social, behavioural, or location graphs.

Unrecorded does not mean unreal.

Private does not mean unimportant.

## 16.8 Preserve plurality of interpretation

Different people and communities may interpret the same object, institution, transformation, relationship, or place differently.

Shared records can preserve provenance and public facts without granting one projection ownership of meaning.

# 18. Canonical Principles

The Pancakes Relational Model adopts the following principles.

1. **Context exceeds representation.**
   Operative conditions exist beyond what any participant or system currently recognizes.

2. **Relations require specified consequence.**
   A useful relational claim identifies its terms, scope, pathway, and effects.

3. **History matters through changed present conditions.**
   Historical carryover does not require a literal memory object or surviving trace artifact.

4. **Relationships are path-dependent relations.**
   A relation becomes a relationship when its prior course helps govern its future course.

5. **Bidirectional consequence is not reciprocal agency.**
   Agency, interpretation, consent, and contestation must be represented separately.

6. **Place is a relational configuration.**
   A relationship with a place is not the whole place.

7. **Recognition may reveal, alter, or constitute relations.**
   Systems should distinguish these operations.

8. **Relation does not automatically create responsibility.**
   Responsibility depends upon power, contribution, capability, commitment, role, benefit, vulnerability, reciprocity, and justice.

9. **Persistence does not prove legitimacy.**
   Historical depth may support care or entrench domination.

10. **Representation must remain proportionate and revisable.**
    Pancakes should make consequential relations legible without turning human life into an exhaustive graph.

# 19. Closing Statement

Participants exist within contexts containing consequential relations.

Interaction can alter the conditions of later interaction.

When that historical carryover helps govern the future course of a relation, the relation becomes path-dependent: a relationship.

Places are materially situated configurations in which many relations and relationships overlap.

Institutions are governed relational configurations that coordinate participation across time.

Artifacts, practices, records, bodies, environments, and symbolic systems may each carry different consequences of what occurred before.

Pancakes should help people recognize, interpret, contest, care for, and transform these relations without confusing representation with reality, history with memory, bidirectional consequence with reciprocal agency, or involvement with justified authority.

The compact form of the theory is:

> **Consequential relations shape present possibilities. Prior interaction may alter those possibilities. When that altered present governs future interaction, the relation has become historically structured.**
