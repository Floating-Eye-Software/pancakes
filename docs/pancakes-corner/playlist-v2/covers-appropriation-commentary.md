# Covers, Appropriation, and the Fantasy of a Universal Playlist

This is a standalone critical commentary on the Pancakes playlists. It is not
one of the fictional viral reviews.

The Pancakes playlists want to tell a generous story about music. Songs travel
across borders, generations, languages, and communities. People hear an old
recording and discover a new way to describe work, survival, pleasure, or home.
That story is real. It is also incomplete. Music does not travel across a flat
world. It moves through unequal industries, unequal audiences, unequal access
to recording technology, and unequal systems of recognition. A playlist that
celebrates connection has to ask who is permitted to borrow, who is expected
to supply the source material, and who gets paid or remembered when a song
crosses into a new market.

The first correction is factual: not every cover in these playlists is a white
person appropriating a non-white artist. The v1 recording of “I Will Survive”
by Cake is a white alternative-rock band reworking a song made iconic by Black
singer Gloria Gaynor. Love and Rockets, also a white British act, recorded
“Ball of Confusion,” a song associated with the Black American group the
Temptations. Sinéad O’Connor, an Irish white artist, recorded Peter Tosh’s
“Downpressor Man,” a song rooted in Jamaican reggae and Rastafarian moral and
political language. Those are important examples of racialized cultural
transfer. But v2 also includes performers such as Miriam Makeba, Master KG
and Nomcebo, Daler Mehndi, Youssou N’Dour, Buena Vista Social Club, The HU,
Israel Kamakawiwoʻole, and Ladysmith Black Mambazo. The playlist is not simply
a catalogue of white appropriation.

That correction should not become an excuse. Covering is not automatically
appropriation, but neither is it automatically innocent exchange. The useful
questions are more demanding than “Did the singer admire the original?” Who
controls the recording? Does the new version acknowledge the song’s history?
Does it preserve or erase the political world that produced it? Does the
covering artist gain access to an audience that the originating artist was
denied? Does the cover translate the song, or does it turn another community’s
struggle into atmosphere?

“Downpressor Man” exposes the problem clearly. O’Connor’s performance is
earnest, powerful, and politically aligned with the song’s condemnation of
oppression. It is not the same thing as a white artist inventing a reggae
persona for novelty. Yet the playlist’s own language can smooth away the
specificity that makes the recording meaningful. Peter Tosh’s Rastafarian
framework and Jamaican political context are not merely portable symbols of
universal resistance. They are histories. A listener can find solidarity in
the song without pretending that solidarity makes the histories interchangeable.

Cake’s “I Will Survive” raises a different issue. The band’s dry, irritated
version changes the emotional temperature of Gloria Gaynor’s disco classic.
That reinterpretation is artistically interesting: survival becomes less like
triumph and more like exhausted refusal. But the original is not a neutral
container waiting for a clever rearrangement. Gaynor’s performance helped make
the song a feminist and LGBTQ+ touchstone, and its meaning was shaped by Black
women’s popular music, disco culture, and queer reception. A white alternative
band can reveal something new in the song, but it also benefits from a canon in
which the white band’s ironic distance can seem like it is trying to be clever
instead of meeting the original’s directness.

“Ball of Confusion” presents the same danger through political language. The
Temptations’ recording belongs to a Black American tradition of naming social
crisis from inside the crisis. Love and Rockets can make the song feel
post-punk, atmospheric, or timeless, but “timeless” is often the word used
when a listener no longer wants to confront the historical specificity of a
protest. A cover may extend a song’s life; it may also make the conditions that
gave the song urgency seem safely distant.

V2’s international range creates a related risk even where no cover is
involved. A sequence can place Punjabi pop, South African dance music, Cuban
music, Mongolian metal, Hawaiian vocal tradition, and European
singer-songwriter music beside one another and call the result “human
flourishing.” The intention may be respectful. The structure can still become
a museum of pleasant difference, where cultures appear as evidence for a thesis
written by someone else. “World music” is not a neutral shelf. It has often
made Western listeners the implied center and everyone else the source of
color, rhythm, wisdom, or authenticity.

There is a more precise version of the “white people with guitars” concern in
v1 itself. At least two tracks place white singer-songwriters in the role of
the authoritative political narrator. Billy Bragg opens the sequence with
“There Is Power in a Union,” a white British musician using a guitar-led folk
form to state the playlist’s first civic premise: collective organization is
the source of power. Sarah Harmer’s “Escarpment Blues” later gives a white
Canadian singer-songwriter a similarly elevated position as witness to land,
development, and environmental responsibility. Both songs can be sincere,
well-made, and politically useful. The question is why their voices are
allowed to frame the playlist’s argument as analysis rather than being heard
primarily as two situated people’s opinions.

This is not an objection to Bragg or Harmer having opinions. It is an objection
to the way curation can turn a particular white folk voice into the transparent
voice of “ordinary people,” “the land,” or “the public good.” Bragg’s song is
not the union speaking. It is Bragg’s interpretation of solidarity, voiced
through a musical tradition that has long been granted seriousness and moral
legibility. Harmer’s song is not the escarpment speaking. It is a specific
environmental intervention, shaped by a particular Canadian context, audience,
and account of what is at stake. The playlist’s liner notes sometimes move too
quickly from those situated works to universal claims about civilization.

The contrast with other tracks makes the imbalance easier to hear. “The
Message” is not presented as a white commentator’s explanation of urban
hardship; it is a report from inside a Black urban experience. “Downpressor
Man,” even in O’Connor’s version, carries the history of Peter Tosh and
Rastafarian political language. The danger is that the white guitar-led tracks
become the interpretive brackets around everyone else: Bragg tells us how to
understand collective struggle, and Harmer tells us how to understand place.
Other artists are then heard as examples within an argument whose authority
has already been established.

That may be privilege in the relevant sense—not a claim that Bragg or Harmer
are uniquely malicious, and not proof that their songs should be removed. It
is the privilege of being heard as an individual whose perspective can stand
for a larger moral truth. The corrective is simple but demanding: name the
speaker, keep the political and geographic context visible, and make room for
other people to describe the union, the city, and the land in their own voices.
The playlist can appreciate guitars while refusing to let a white guitar
become the neutral microphone of civilization.

The answer is not to prohibit listening across difference. That would turn
cultural boundaries into prisons and deny the long history of influence,
translation, migration, collaboration, and reinvention. The answer is to make
the listening more accountable. Name the source. Keep the original performer
visible. Do not describe a tradition as ancient and pure when its artists are
actively changing it. Do not treat a marginalized artist’s political work as a
decorative mood. Follow the money when a cover becomes more famous than the
original. Ask what the new performance adds, and what it risks taking away.

Pancakes v1 and v2 are strongest when they understand music as situated human
work rather than universal emotional currency. The playlists should therefore
keep their connections, but revise their confidence. No song becomes common
property merely because it moves us. A shared world is not one in which
everyone is allowed to take from everyone else. It is one in which people can
meet, borrow, answer, and transform while remaining accountable to the lives,
communities, and histories that made the music possible.
