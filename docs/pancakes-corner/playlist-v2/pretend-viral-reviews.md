# If Pancakes Playlist v1 and v2 Went Viral

These are fictional, speculative reviews written from distinct public
perspectives. They are not real reviews, endorsements, quotations, or
imitations of any named publication or person. The playlist descriptions and
liner notes are the source for the musical details; the reactions imagine what
different audiences might hear in the two sequences.

## 1. The Feminist Influencer: “The Playlist That Finally Counts the Work”

I have been waiting for someone to make a playlist that understands the
connection between “women’s work,” survival, and joy without turning the whole
thing into a beige self-care carousel. Pancakes v1 starts with the union and
then keeps widening the frame: the city, the home, the body, the institution,
the closet, the road, the land. That sequencing matters. It says exploitation
is not one bad boss or one bad boyfriend. It is a pattern that teaches us to
call somebody else’s labor natural.

The Paris Paloma into Dolly Parton pairing is the obvious clip, and yes, it
will become a viral carousel with captions about emotional labor and “being
the default adult.” But the smarter move is what comes afterward. *9 to 5*
doesn’t let us leave the office and congratulate ourselves for recognizing
domestic labor. *Institutionalized* asks who gets to define a person’s reality.
*Rehab* asks whether rejecting control is the same thing as being free. Then
*Smalltown Boy* shows what happens when the community makes safety conditional
on conformity.

That is a lot of politics for one playlist, but it never feels like homework.
The songs have hooks, swagger, melodrama, and enough contradiction to keep the
listener from standing outside them with a clipboard. *Deceptacon* is
especially important because it calls out rebellion as branding. We have all
watched corporations borrow feminist language while making women work longer
for less. A playlist that includes that critique and then ends with *Bread* is
making a radical demand: liberation has to reach the kitchen, the nervous
system, and the appetite.

V2 is where I stopped expecting a manifesto and started crying. It asks what
all the struggle is for. Not productivity. Not a more efficient personal
brand. Not becoming invulnerable. The answer is music after midnight, dancing
without embarrassment, old stories, travel, friendship, children, neighbors,
and a sunrise that nobody has monetized yet. *Jerusalema*, *Chan Chan*, *Hello
My Baby*, *Northern Sky*, and *Sunrise* create a politics of ordinary pleasure.

My only warning is that the internet will try to flatten this into an
aesthetic: union-core, bread-core, feminist civilization-core. Don’t let it.
Listen in order. Notice who is working, who is waiting, who is leaving, and who
finally gets to come home. Then ask whether the people doing the invisible work
in your life are getting any of the joy the playlist promises. That is the
real review prompt.

The playlist also understands that anger is not a personality. It can be a
signal, a boundary, a form of memory, or the first evidence that a person has
noticed the arrangement is unfair. The later songs give that anger somewhere
to go: toward land, voice, friendship, appetite, and a home that does not
require self-erasure.

The project’s most persuasive ethical claim is also its most practical one:
people should not have to become symbols in order to deserve care. The songs
keep returning to bodies, rooms, roads, and meals. That scale prevents the
politics from becoming purely performative.

## 2. The MAGA Podcast: “They Had Us at the Bread”

Let’s be honest: when somebody sends you a playlist called Pancakes and the
first song is about unions, you expect a two-hour lecture about how America is
the problem. I almost deleted it. Then I listened to the whole thing, and the
strangest thing happened: I found myself agreeing with more of it than I
expected.

Now, there is plenty here that the people who make this playlist would not
like us to say. *Downpressor Man* sounds like a sermon against corrupt power.
*The Message* is a hard look at cities where families get trapped by broken
institutions. *Labour* goes straight after the way households can turn one
person into a servant and call it love. And *Deceptacon* has that familiar
left-wing suspicion of anything that looks too polished or commercial.

But hold on. The deeper argument is not “government should run your life.” It
is almost the opposite. The playlist keeps asking what institutions are for,
and whether they are accountable to ordinary people. That is a question many
working Americans already understand. We know what it feels like when a
bureaucrat, a school, a corporation, or a media company decides it knows us
better than we know ourselves.

The sequence also respects work. It does not pretend everybody can float above
material reality on good vibes. It starts with solidarity, moves through
housing, wages, family responsibility, illness, and escape, and ends with
something that sounds almost offensively simple: people need enough safety to
rest, enough food to eat, and enough room for small pleasures. That is not a
radical idea. That is what a decent country should deliver.

V2 is even more persuasive because it stops yelling. *Green Onions* is people
doing a job well. *These Boots Are Made for Walkin’* is a boundary. *Weapon of
Choice* is an older guy dancing like nobody can tell him he is too old. *Pata
Pata* and *Jerusalema* are proof that culture travels because people enjoy it,
not because a committee needs to diversify a spreadsheet. *The HU* makes the
same point with tradition: modern does not have to mean ashamed of the past.

Here is where I still disagree with the Pancakes worldview. The playlist is too
ready to see power as suspect and too willing to turn every social problem into
a system. Sometimes people make bad choices. Sometimes families, churches,
and neighborhoods—not another program—are what save them. But I will give the
creators this: they understand that institutions should protect the real life
people have inherited, built, and loved.

And yes, the last song on v1 is about bread. We mocked it on the show. Then we
played it twice. That is how they get you.

The surprising lesson is that cultural politics does not have to begin with a
flag or a slogan. It can begin with the question of whether a child, a worker,
or an old neighbor has enough room to live normally. We may not share the
playlist makers’ theory of the state, but we can recognize the common-sense
standard underneath it: if the system produces fear, dependency, and loneliness
for ordinary people, somebody has to answer for the result.

## 3. The New York Times-Style Critic: “The Playlist as Civic Argument”

The viral success of Pancakes v1 and v2 would be easy to misread as a triumph
of eclectic taste. Their selections range from Billy Bragg to Grandmaster
Flash, Nina Simone to Yaelokre, J. J. Cale to Daler Mehndi. But the achievement
is not breadth alone. It is the construction of a sequence that behaves like
an essay while retaining the pleasures of a mixtape.

The first volume begins in collective struggle. *There Is Power in a Union*
establishes solidarity as a condition of dignity; *Downpressor Man* enlarges
that premise into moral accountability. From there, the playlist moves through
urban precarity, unpaid domestic labor, waged work, institutional coercion,
addiction, queer exile, and the compromised forms of survival that follow.
Its argument is cumulative rather than programmatic. The listener is asked to
notice how public and private systems echo one another.

The middle of the sequence is particularly deft. Paris Paloma’s *Labour* and
Dolly Parton’s *9 to 5* could have produced a predictable contrast between
domestic and corporate exploitation. Instead, the movement into *Institutionalized*
and *Rehab* complicates the language of care itself. Institutions can rescue,
diagnose, discipline, and silence. Refusal may be necessary, but it is not
automatically liberating. The playlist’s politics are strongest where they
allow these propositions to remain in tension.

Its final turn—from Tracy Chapman’s constrained aspiration through Sarah
Harmer’s ecological attention, Nina Simone’s reclamation, Lauryn Hill’s
generational patience, and Yaelokre’s fable of belonging—refuses the
conventional climax of triumph. The coda, Anya Nami’s *Bread*, is almost
comic in its modesty. Yet its modesty is the point. A just social order should
leave room for appetite, rest, and pleasure that has not been translated into
achievement.

V2 begins where v1 ends: with the question of what institutions are meant to
make possible. Its opening tracks offer competence without spectacle. *After
Midnight* and *Green Onions* are studies in ease; the musicians sound as if
mastery has become ordinary. The sequence then travels through history,
confusion, confidence, dance, translation, memory, tradition, home, and
celebration. If v1 is a diagnosis, v2 is an account of the life diagnosis is
supposed to serve.

There are moments when the project’s civilizational language risks overreach.
A playlist cannot repair the institutions it critiques, and its cosmopolitan
range occasionally approaches the danger of treating distinct traditions as
evidence for one universal thesis. Still, Pancakes succeeds at an increasingly
rare cultural task: it makes pleasure feel politically serious without making
it solemn. The result is less a soundtrack for a movement than a proposal for
what movements might be trying to protect.

The playlists also resist the critic’s usual temptation to rank seriousness
above pleasure. Their most durable insight is formal: political meaning can be
carried by sequence, tempo, and release as much as by explicit words. The
listener experiences the argument in the body before deciding whether to agree
with it intellectually.

Its success also depends on the discipline of the order. Rearranged as a set of
singles, the songs would make familiar claims. In sequence, they produce a
different kind of knowledge: each new track revises the listener’s assumption
about what the previous one meant.

## 4. CBC Arts and Current Affairs: “From Protest to the Places We Keep”

Pancakes v1 and v2 have gone viral because they offer listeners something the
algorithm usually avoids: a beginning, a middle, and a reason to stay until the
end. They are not simply collections of songs with a shared mood. They are two
acts in a conversation about public life, and they have prompted the sort of
cross-country discussion that begins in music threads and ends in family
kitchens, union halls, classrooms, and community radio studios.

The first playlist has a particularly recognizable Canadian resonance in its
attention to care as infrastructure. Sarah Harmer’s *Escarpment Blues* arrives
after songs about labor, poverty, institutional authority, and escape, and it
changes the scale of the conversation. The land is not scenery behind social
life. It is part of the system that makes social life possible: water, habitat,
memory, and the places people know by walking through them.

That does not make the sequence narrowly national. *The Message*, *Smalltown
Boy*, *Fast Car*, and *Ain’t Got No, I Got Life* connect local experience to
larger histories of urban pressure, queer displacement, economic constraint,
and survival. The playlist’s most valuable contribution is its refusal to
separate those conditions into neat categories. Home, work, identity, health,
and environment overlap in ordinary lives.

V2 is gentler but not less political. It asks what all those institutions,
technologies, markets, and political arguments are supposed to protect. The
answer arrives through sound rather than policy language: an evening among
friends, a groove played with quiet competence, a traditional song kept alive,
a dance shared across borders, a remembered neighborhood, a morning light.

The sequence’s international reach is part of its appeal. *Tunak Tunak Tun*,
*Pata Pata*, *Jerusalema*, *Chan Chan*, *Yuve Yuve Yu*, and the multilingual
*7 Seconds* offer a map of connection that is not based on sameness. The songs
do not erase their origins in order to become global. They travel with their
particularity intact.

Some listeners will find the project’s language of flourishing too polished,
especially when set against songs that describe harm with much less distance.
Others will wonder whether celebration can carry the weight placed on it. But
the playlists answer that concern by keeping joy embodied. This is not a demand
that anyone feel grateful. It is a reminder that public choices become
meaningful in the private details they permit: staying up late, cooking, caring
for a neighbor, hearing an old song, watching the sunrise.

The viral question is no longer “What does Pancakes mean?” It is “Which song
made you call somebody?” That may be the most Canadian outcome of all: a
playlist becoming an excuse to talk across the table.

There is also a useful regional humility in the project’s notion of scale. It
does not imagine that a national institution must solve every human need. It
asks whether large systems can leave enough room for local knowledge, inherited
places, and the informal networks that make public life feel inhabited rather
than administered. That is a question worth carrying beyond the playlist.

The playlist is also a small argument against the idea that a public culture
must choose between critique and consolation. It can tell the truth about
extraction and still preserve a place for dancing. In fact, the dancing may be
part of how the truth remains bearable enough to act on.

## 5. The Gen-Z Music Curator: “The Algorithm Accidentally Developed a Soul”

Pancakes v1 is the rare playlist that makes you listen in order without making
you feel like you are doing assigned reading. It starts with “union anthem,”
then immediately gets complicated: oppression, city stress, domestic labor,
office exploitation, institutions deciding who you are, addiction, queer
escape, feminist punk, survival, climate, selfhood, and then—somehow—bread.

Naturally, the internet has already split the tracks into micro-aesthetics.
There is a *Labour* / *9 to 5* slideshow. There is a “songs for leaving your
small town” edit built around *Smalltown Boy* and *Fast Car*. Somebody made a
video of doing dishes to *Deceptacon* with the caption “my unpaid labor has a
breakdown.” The bread song is everywhere. Nobody can explain why the bread song
is everywhere.

But the viral format misses what makes the playlist good: the transitions. It
doesn’t just collect songs that say “I am oppressed” or “I am healed.” It lets
people be inconsistent. The person who wants freedom can still need help. The
person who distrusts institutions can still be in danger. Survival can be
funny, bitter, stylish, exhausted, and hopeful in the same hour.

Then v2 arrives like the unexpected second season that changes the genre. The
first volume is “what is wrong with the world?” V2 is “okay, but what are we
trying to save?” *Green Onions* makes competence feel hot. *Weapon of Choice*
proves that cool is a physical skill. *Tunak Tunak Tun* is pure internet
history, except the playlist treats it as more than a meme. *Jerusalema* turns
a pandemic dance into a record of people insisting on being together.

The best tracks are the ones that make the comment section unexpectedly
serious. People argue about whether *Chan Chan* sounds like home even when they
have never been to Cuba. They post grandparents dancing to *Pata Pata*. They
talk about how *7 Seconds* sounds like a conversation before it sounds like a
message. Someone explains throat singing under a video of *The HU*. Someone
else says their dad played *Northern Sky* after funerals.

That is the actual viral phenomenon: not the playlist becoming a brand, but
listeners attaching their own memories to it. V2 understands that joy is not
content. It is something people recognize together.

My only complaint is that Pancakes is too confident that every song has one
job in the civilization story. Songs escape their assigned meanings the
second listeners use them. But maybe that is the point. The playlist gives us
a route, and the internet covers it in side quests. Five stars. No skips. Eat
some bread.

Also, the playlist is weird in the best way. It trusts listeners to move from
political anger to a Mongolian rock band, from a pandemic dance to a Cuban
guitar line, without demanding a thesis statement for every transition. That
trust is probably why people are sharing it. The sequence leaves enough empty
space for someone else’s memory to enter.

## 6. The Labor Organizer: “A Mixtape for the Bargaining Table”

The first time I played Pancakes v1 at a union meeting, I expected people to
make fun of the premise. We are used to campaign songs that tell us to keep
fighting. We are less used to a sequence that asks what fighting is meant to
produce.

The opening track, *There Is Power in a Union*, is direct about collective
strength. It is a useful beginning because organizing is not a mood. It is a
practice of making power visible where employers prefer to see isolated
individuals. But Pancakes does not stop at the workplace. *The Message* moves
through housing, policing, debt, family pressure, and incarceration. *Labour*
names the unpaid work that makes paid work possible. *9 to 5* reminds us that
formal employment can still be a structure for theft.

That sequence belongs in every steward training session because it expands the
question “What are the working conditions?” Working conditions include the
commute, the child-care arrangement, the medical appointment, the fear of
being disbelieved, the expectation to perform gratitude, and the exhaustion
that follows somebody home. A contract can be legally valid and still leave a
person with no life.

The songs about institutions are especially useful. *Institutionalized* is a
warning about the moment an organization stops hearing a person’s account and
starts treating its own categories as reality. *Rehab* adds a harder lesson:
opposition to coercion does not remove the need for care. Good organizing has to
hold both truths. People deserve voice and agency, and people can also be hurt,
unwell, or unsafe.

V1’s late tracks keep the settlement honest. *Fast Car* asks what mobility
means when wages and relationships cannot support the dream. *Escarpment Blues*
reminds us that extraction is not only something a company does to workers; it
can damage the places workers and families depend on. *Bread* is the best
possible coda because it translates the whole argument into a bargaining
question: after the campaign, can people eat, sleep, laugh, and enjoy one
another?

V2 is the playlist I would play after a successful contract ratification. It
does not declare victory so much as describe the life victory should make
possible. *Green Onions* is craft. *Pata Pata* is collective pleasure.
*Jerusalema* is coordination without a boss. *Chan Chan* is time outside the
clock. *Hello My Baby* is voices answering one another. *Sunrise* and
*Celebration* make the final case: leisure is not a reward for becoming more
productive. It is part of the purpose of work.

If this playlist is viral, bring it to the picket line, the break room, and the
negotiating committee. Then ask the only question that matters: who gets the
life these songs describe?

That question changes how we judge a settlement. A raise matters, but so does
whether the new schedule lets a parent attend school pickup. A safety clause
matters, but so does whether workers can use it without retaliation. A strong
contract is not only a document that distributes money; it is a device for
returning time, voice, and possibility to the people whose work created value.

## 7. The Public-Radio Cultural Essayist: “Who Gets to Go Home?”

The most revealing word in Pancakes is not “playlist.” It is “home.” The two
volumes approach the word from opposite directions. V1 asks why people are
forced away from safety, dignity, recognition, and material security. V2 asks
what a society would have to preserve so that coming home could be an ordinary
pleasure rather than a lucky ending.

The first volume’s journey is outward and downward. It begins with organized
labor and descends into the ordinary pressures that official histories tend to
separate: the city’s fear, the household’s unpaid work, the office’s stolen
time, the institution’s interpretation, the family or community that cannot
make room for difference. By the time *Smalltown Boy* arrives, exile feels less
like a dramatic plot event than the accumulated result of many small refusals.

Yet the playlist does not romanticize escape. *Fast Car* is a dream made
credible by work and then narrowed by poverty, care, and disappointment. The
road does not automatically lead to freedom. A person needs somewhere to go,
and someone willing to build the conditions of arrival.

That is why *Escarpment Blues* matters so much. The place one returns to is not
an abstract backdrop. It has water, species, memory, and contested futures. To
protect home is to protect the relationships that make a place habitable.

V2 changes the lighting. Its first tracks notice what a flourishing life feels
like before it becomes a theory: the unhurried groove of *After Midnight*, the
quiet precision of *Green Onions*, the pleasure of a good song being played
well. From there it moves through history and confusion toward dance,
translation, memory, tradition, and morning.

The sequence is at its most moving when it refuses to treat global connection
as the cancellation of local belonging. *Tunak Tunak Tun*, *Pata Pata*,
*Jerusalema*, *Chan Chan*, and *Yuve Yuve Yu* remain distinct songs with
distinct cultural histories. They do not become interchangeable examples of
“world music.” Their presence makes a more modest claim: human beings can meet
without first becoming the same.

The final run—*Northern Sky*, *Sunrise*, *Somewhere Over the Rainbow / What a
Wonderful World*, and *Celebration*—could easily become sentimental. Instead,
it feels like an earned softness. The listener has been reminded that
civilization is not judged only by its monuments or crises. It is judged by
whether people can linger, remember, make music, raise children, care for
neighbors, and experience beauty without needing to justify the expense.

Pancakes went viral because it gives the culture-war internet a rare object of
disagreement that is also an invitation. People argue about politics, genre,
translation, and taste. Then they send the playlist to somebody they miss.
That gesture may be the clearest review of all: a home is partly the place, and
partly the song that helps us recognize one another there.

## 8. The Cable-News Nationalist Pundit: “Stop Romanticizing Everywhere Else”

Let me save you twenty minutes of pretending this is sophisticated. Pancakes
v2 is a playlist made by people who think civilization is a group hug. They
take songs from countries most Americans could not find on a map, add a few
old favorites from home, and announce that all cultures are equally beautiful
if we would just dance together. That is not analysis. That is tourism with a
soundtrack.

The defenders will say the sequence is about craftsmanship, memory, tradition,
and ordinary pleasure. Fine. Nobody is against pleasure. The problem is the
politics hiding underneath the pleasantness. *Pata Pata*, *Jerusalema*, *Chan
Chan*, *Yuve Yuve Yu*—each song is treated as evidence that every place has a
special wisdom the West has somehow forgotten. We are expected to admire the
rhythm and ignore the institutions, corruption, poverty, instability, and
authoritarianism that make those countries less attractive places to live.

This is the classic elite fantasy: turn the rest of the world into a series of
colorful postcards, then feel morally superior for appreciating them. The
playlist’s creators would never say that directly. They use words like
“particularity,” “translation,” and “belonging.” But the effect is the same.
America becomes the guilty party, while every foreign society is allowed to
appear as pure community, ancient tradition, or joyful resistance.

And where is the gratitude? *Jerusalema* became a global dance during the
pandemic, but the playlist frames that as humanity connecting across borders.
Maybe it was also people using an American-built internet, American social
platforms, and modern technology that our civilization developed. *The HU*
combines Mongolian tradition with rock, and suddenly we are told tradition is
alive because it changes. Yet when our own traditions change, these same
people call it cultural collapse.

The hypocrisy is almost musical. The playlist wants the comfort of a wealthy,
stable civilization while condemning the confidence and national pride that
built one. It wants global culture without borders, but it still expects
someone to maintain the roads, enforce the laws, defend the territory, and pay
for the public goods. The people who made this playlist will call that
“extraction.” Most families call it keeping the lights on.

Here is the truth nobody in the comment section wants to hear: not every place
is equally safe, prosperous, or free. Some countries work better than others.
Some institutions deserve loyalty because they protect the ordinary life this
playlist claims to value. If you cannot say that without apologizing, you are
not defending civilization. You are collecting aesthetic evidence against it.

I will admit one thing. *Celebration* is catchy. *Green Onions* is good. And
the final stretch has a certain emotional effect, especially if you are already
inclined to believe that the world is one big neighborhood. But a civilization
cannot be governed by vibes, and a nation cannot be run like a multicultural
playlist. Admire the songs if you want. Just do not confuse a pleasant chorus
with proof that every society offers the same bargain.

Perhaps that is why the playlist has travelled so far beyond music culture. It
does not ask listeners to agree on a single politics of belonging. It asks them
to notice the conditions under which belonging becomes possible, and to hear
the difference between a home that shelters a person and a home that merely
claims ownership of them.
