# Pancakes Playlist v3: Liner Notes

[Back to the playlist](pancakes-playlist-v3.md) | [Back to the v3 index](index.md)

## Introduction

The first *Pancakes* playlist asked what institutions must protect.

The second asked what civilization is for.

The third begins with the largest possible answer: imagine the divisions of
the existing world swept away. Its first ten tracks formed a complete sequence,
ending with *Mr. Roboto*. Then, a week later, an accordion sounded from a
Finnish harvest festival.

Songs pass from one writer to another performer. Lennon is sung by Fiona Apple
and Siouxsie Sioux. George Harrison's lament becomes the setting for Prince's
astonishing act of interpretation. The Rolling Stones are dismantled and
rebuilt by Devo. Trent Reznor's confession reaches old age in Johnny Cash's
voice. A folk melody survives centuries of migration, parody, performance, and
internet rediscovery.

The covers are not detours from the playlist's meaning. They are its method.
Culture does not endure by remaining untouched. It endures because people
receive it, alter it, argue with it, and pass it on.

The sequence also tells a darker story. Idealism becomes introspection;
introspection becomes a place where someone can get lost; exhaustion seeks
sedation; manufactured desire gives way to emptiness; private emptiness becomes
an old man's reckoning; and that reckoning widens into a metropolis that has
built isolation into its architecture.

[*The Forty-Seventh Frequency*](the-forty-seventh-frequency.md), the companion
story to the playlists, was written when the complete sequence contained
forty-six songs. Readers may want to experience the story before returning to
these notes; nothing below reveals its plot.

*Ievan polkka* was added a week later. It is therefore not merely the next song
in the sequence but a coda written after the story was already complete: a
particular performer, at a particular gathering, playing a melody that has
survived worlds of change. It does not revise the story's events. It changes
what we are permitted to hope for after reaching its final page.

This is not a playlist about reaching utopia. It is about what remains audible
while people try.

---

## 01. John Lennon — *Imagine*

The playlist opens with an act of subtraction. Lennon asks the listener to
remove, one by one, the inherited structures through which people organize
identity, property, loyalty, conflict, and hope. The song's power comes from
making the resulting world feel emotionally available before it has been made
institutionally plausible.

For *Pancakes*, that is both invitation and warning. A moral horizon matters:
we need some way to picture peace before we can build toward it. But imagining
the absence of harmful institutions is not the same as designing trustworthy
ones. Food still has to be grown, care organized, disputes resolved, memory
preserved, and power restrained. The rest of v3 takes place inside that gap
between the beautiful proposition and the difficult work.

## 02. Fiona Apple — *Across the Universe*

The second track keeps Lennon but changes the weather around him. Fiona Apple's
performance is intimate and self-possessed, while the *Pleasantville* video
places that composure amid escalating disorder. The song can sound serene, but
the scene makes serenity ambiguous. Is the singer accepting the world,
enduring it, or protecting an interior life from its noise?

That ambiguity advances the playlist. *Imagine* addresses humanity as a whole;
*Across the Universe* asks what a single consciousness can do while the whole
world keeps happening. Apple does not merely preserve Lennon's song. Her voice
changes the problem inside it. Transmission is already interpretation.

## 03. Siouxsie and the Banshees — *Dear Prudence*

The song is about getting lost in idealistic introspection. Going inward can
look like peace, wisdom, or transcendence from the inside, while becoming an
absence noticed by everyone outside. Prudence does not need her inner world
denounced. She needs reminding that there is also a world beyond it.

After Apple's still center, the song is a gentle call back into relationship.
No one is ordered to recover, perform happiness, or explain an absence. The
door is simply opened. Notice the person who has withdrawn, keep a place for
them, and let invitation do what force cannot.

## 04. Prince, Tom Petty, Jeff Lynne, Steve Winwood, Dhani Harrison, and others — *While My Guitar Gently Weeps*

George Harrison's song begins from a painful contradiction: love is present as
a possibility and neglected as a practice. The 2004 Rock and Roll Hall of Fame
performance answers that loneliness with a crowded stage. Friends, heirs, and
fellow musicians hold the composition together until Prince steps forward and
turns the guitar solo into something almost gravitational.

The moment is spectacular, but it is not a conquest of the song. Prince's
individual brilliance occurs inside an act of collective remembrance, and the
performance belongs to the whole arrangement even while he briefly changes
its center of mass. This is stewardship at its most joyful: inherit something,
bring everything you have to it, and leave it more alive than you found it.

## 05. Ramones — *I Wanna Be Sedated*

Then the nervous system gives out.

The Ramones make overload funny by reducing the desired future to chemical
quiet. Motion, schedule, repetition, and confinement have become so oppressive
that relief no longer means freedom or pleasure. It means not having to remain
fully conscious for the next demand.

Placed after the grandeur of the Hall of Fame performance, the track punctures
any temptation to let culture become only monuments and virtuosity. Art is
also made by exhausted people in vans, airports, hotels, workplaces, and rooms
they cannot wait to leave. A civilization that produces marvels while burning
through the people who make and maintain them has misunderstood its own
achievement.

## 06. Devo — *(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction*

Devo does not cover the Rolling Stones so much as take the song apart on a work
bench. The original's swagger becomes a series of jerks, alarms, and failed
instructions. Desire no longer sounds rebellious. It sounds programmed—and
the program is malfunctioning.

This is the playlist's most explicit demonstration that a cover can be an
argument. The composition remains recognizable, but its assumptions have been
reversed. After the Ramones seek escape through sedation, Devo locates the
distress in a system that manufactures wanting and then makes satisfaction
impossible. The robotic performance also plants a signal that *Mr. Roboto*
will answer later: what happens to a person who must move in the machine's
rhythm without becoming one?

## 07. The Cranberries — *Empty*

The pressure suddenly falls away, and what remains is not peace.

Dolores O'Riordan gives emptiness weight. The song is not about lacking one
identifiable object that could simply be restored. It is about realizing that
something organizing a life has vanished, leaving the person unable to locate
either the loss or the self that existed before it.

In the sequence, this is the emotional consequence hidden inside the two
previous tracks. Sedation can stop sensation; consumer systems can keep desire
circulating; neither can tell a person what the wanting was for. The song clears
the room before *Hurt* enters it.

## 08. Johnny Cash — *Hurt*

Trent Reznor's song changes when sung by Johnny Cash near the end of his life.
Words that once belonged to young self-destruction acquire age, history,
bereavement, public memory, and the visible ruins of success. The recording is
still a cover, but it feels less like possession than recognition: one artist
hears a truth in another artist's work and discovers that he has grown into it.

The video also reaches backward into the first *Pancakes* playlist. It is easy
to imagine the old man seated among the remains of wealth in *Hurt* as the same
man who sat across the table from Paris Paloma in *Labour*, fifty years later.
The earlier scene contains only those two people at the table, which makes the
imbalance more intimate. One person is present; the other does not truly see
her. Later, the table has grown vast, the possessions remain, and the same man
confronts an isolation they cannot repair.

That imagined continuity is not a tidy punishment, and regret is not the same
as restitution. It is a warning about the poverty inside domination: a person
can spend a lifetime consuming other people's care and still arrive at the end
without having learned how to receive love.

## 09. Anne Clark — *Sleeper in Metropolis*

*Hurt* can be heard as one person's reckoning. *Sleeper in Metropolis* widens
the frame until private alienation becomes an environmental condition. Anne
Clark's spoken voice moves through an electronic city that regulates distance,
rhythm, labor, sleep, and contact. Loneliness is no longer only a feeling
someone carries. It has been built into the habitat.

This is where v3 returns most clearly to the institutional questions of the
first playlist. Environments train behavior even when no single authority
issues a command. A city can connect millions of people while arranging their
lives as solitude. The sleeper is not outside the system and cannot simply
walk away from it; the system is the medium through which waking, work, desire,
and escape have to occur.

## 10. Styx — *Mr. Roboto*

The apparent novelty song is carrying an entire small dystopia. In the story of
*Kilroy Was Here*, rock performer Robert Orin Charles Kilroy is imprisoned by
an authoritarian movement and escapes by hiding inside a robot disguise. The
famous declaration of secret identity is therefore literal: machinery is the
mask a human being uses to survive a system that has outlawed his voice.

The name reaches further back. “Kilroy was here” was a piece of anonymous
graffiti carried through the Second World War—a repeated assertion that some
ordinary, unseen person had passed through this place. Styx turns that folk
trace into a named fugitive, then puts him inside a manufactured shell.

After Anne Clark's metropolis, the theatrical excess makes sense. When a
system treats people as components, one survival strategy is to perform the
component perfectly while preserving a self underneath.

As the final track in v3's original ten-song sequence, *Mr. Roboto* leaves us
with the human voice still present inside the machinery. Whether the disguise
is protection, imprisonment, or both remains unresolved. That ambiguity also
makes the song a fitting point of contact with *The Forty-Seventh Frequency*,
whose plot the listener should encounter for themselves.

## 11 / 47. Slack Bird — *Ievan polkka* (live, 2018)

Then, one week after the sequence seemed complete, another song appears.

The coda is a live performance from Kurpitsatalon Sadonkorjuujuhla on September
9, 2018. That date matters because the playlist video is not the later 2023
studio release. What we hear is situated and immediate: Slack Bird, an
accordion, an audience, a named gathering, and a melody whose life began long
before any of them.

This is the sequence's oldest song and newest act of transmission. A regional
dance melody became a comic song, moved through singers and arrangements,
crossed borders, acquired nonsense syllables and memes, and arrived here still
available for another performer to inhabit. No version cancels the others. The
tune survives through variation.

The circumstances of the addition matter. *Ievan polkka* was not selected to
complete the story's design; the story had already been written. The track
arrived afterward and became an answer of a different kind—not an explanation
of the plot or a revision of its ending, but evidence that a finished work can
still acquire a coda. The silence after an ending need not be the final silence.

As a coda, *Ievan polkka* quietly revises *Imagine*. The answer to division is
not necessarily a world scrubbed of all particularity. It may be a world in
which particular places, languages, traditions, and gatherings can flourish
without being turned into weapons against one another.

The playlist begins by imagining that the structures dividing humanity have
vanished. The coda offers one modest structure still worth hoping for: people
meet at harvest time, somebody brings an instrument, an old song is played
again, and everyone present helps carry it forward. If hope can survive even
at the end of everything, perhaps this is what it sounds like.
