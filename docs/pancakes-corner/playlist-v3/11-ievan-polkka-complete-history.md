# *Ievan Polkka*: A Complete History of the Folk Tune, the Comic Song, and the Meme

## From a Finnish-Russian borderland melody to Eino Kettunen, Loituma, Leekspin, Hatsune Miku, and the vibing cat

*Fact-checked research revision: 9 August 2026*

*Ievan Polkka* has been called an old Finnish folk song, a Loituma song, the Leekspin song, and a Hatsune Miku song. Each description catches one period in its life and mistakes that period for the whole.

The object now known around the world as *Ievan Polkka* was not created all at once. It accumulated:

- a travelling dance melody with roots in southeastern Finland and western Russia;
- a comic narrative written by Eino Kettunen and printed in 1928;
- a postwar pairing of those words with the melody now regarded as canonical;
- an a cappella arrangement made by four Finnish folk musicians in the early 1990s;
- a few seconds of improvised syllables extracted from that arrangement in 2006;
- a four-frame anime loop, a misidentified vegetable, a virtual singer, a darbuka player, and a nodding cat.

Its history is therefore not a straight line from “ancient folk song” to “internet meme.” It is a history of repeated recomposition. At every stage, somebody inherited only part of the song and made that part newly useful.

## 1. The name

The title means **“Ieva’s Polka.”** *Ieva* is an eastern Finnish dialect form of *Eeva*, the Finnish equivalent of Eva or Eve; *Ievan* is the possessive form. *Polkka* is the Finnish spelling of polka.

The initial letter is a capital **I**, not a lowercase **l**. The extremely common title *Levan Polkka* is usually explained as a sans-serif capital *I* being misread as a lowercase *l*, after which files, videos, and reposts reinforced the error. That explanation is plausible but not traceable to a single documented first mistake. The dialectal spelling *Ievan polokka* is also found in Finnish records. “Ieva’s Polka” is a reasonable English translation, but “Ievan’s Polka” is grammatically redundant: the Finnish ending already supplies the possessive.

The song is also associated with the title *Savitaipaleen polkka*, after the South Karelian municipality where the melody is treated as local traditional repertoire. It is not the same tune as the still more famous *Säkkijärven polkka*, although the similar names, eastern Finnish associations, and accordion traditions cause them to be confused.

## 2. Before Ieva: the melody

There is no documented moment when someone composed the familiar tune.

[Loituma’s own historical account](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/) says that its folk roots can be traced to the late eighteenth century, especially in southeastern Finland, and that the melody also appears under the name *Savitaipaleen polkka*. Other accounts call the two tunes “very similar” rather than simply giving one melody two names. That discrepancy is normal in oral repertoire, where variants do not always divide neatly into “same tune” and “different tune.” Melodic relatives have also been identified in western Russia, particularly the Smolensk-area dance tune *Smolenski gusatšok* (“the little gander of Smolensk”). This places the tune in a borderland rather than inside a sealed national tradition.

That account requires two qualifications. First, “known since the end of the eighteenth century” is a tradition-based historical claim, not a reference to a surviving eighteenth-century score of the tune in its modern form. Second, the polka as a named dance and musical genre arose in nineteenth-century Bohemia and spread rapidly across Europe. A melody or melodic ancestor may be older, but it cannot have existed in precisely the form of a Finnish “polka” before the polka itself arrived. The safest reconstruction is therefore:

1. a melodic family circulated across southeastern Finland and western Russia;
2. musicians adapted it to newer nineteenth-century couple-dance practice;
3. the resulting polka circulated orally under local names, including *Savitaipaleen polkka*;
4. only later did it become firmly joined to Kettunen’s story about Ieva.

The resemblance to Russian material does not prove a single direction of borrowing. Popular online summaries often add that Russian troops once outnumbered civilians in parts of the border region and then present the soldiers as the melody’s transmission mechanism. The military setting makes cultural exchange plausible, but no source located for this article connects this particular tune to a named unit, musician, or act of borrowing. Tunes can cross borders through soldiers, migrant workers, itinerant musicians, dances, markets, marriages, and ordinary listening. Oral tradition tends to preserve use better than provenance. The melody is old; the evidence for a single birthplace is not strong enough to award it one.

Nor does the evidence support the claim that the tune spent more than a century as a purely instrumental, unnamed accordion piece. The accordion itself belongs to the nineteenth-century part of the story, and a gap in documentation cannot establish that a melody had no names, words, or other instruments in oral use. What can safely be said is that the melody circulated as dance repertoire before its now-familiar union with Kettunen’s text.

## 3. Eino Kettunen gives the tune a story, 1928

Eino Kettunen was born in Juuka in North Karelia in 1894 and died in Helsinki in 1964. He wrote comic songs and popular lyrics in the Savo-Karelian vernacular. His lasting contribution to *Ievan Polkka* was not the traditional melody but the words.

The documentary anchor is a tiny publication. The [Finna catalogue record for Kettunen’s ninth booklet](https://www.finna.fi/Record/jykdok.1286293) describes an eight-page pamphlet called *Eino Kettusen savo-karjalaisia y.m. humoristisia lauluja. 9:s vihko*—roughly, “Eino Kettunen’s Savo-Karelian and Other Humorous Songs, Booklet 9.” It was self-published in Lieksa and printed by Kirjapaino Hermes in Tampere in 1928. The catalogue classifies it as *arkkiveisut*: printed song sheets or inexpensive song pamphlets.

That format matters. An *arkkiveisu* stood between print and oral culture. Such sheets were sold as news, amusement, romance, satire, or social commentary, usually expecting readers to fit new words to a melody they already knew. The [Finnish Music Quarterly’s account of the form](https://www.fmq.fi/articles/contemporary-folk-song) describes these “sheet songs” as printed works sold to the public for information and entertainment. Kettunen was not collecting anonymous village words. He was participating in a popular publishing system that deliberately placed new authorship on top of shared musical memory.

### What the song says

The title character is not the narrator. A young man tells the story of a night when the sound of a polka makes his feet itch to dance. Ieva’s mother is watching her, but Ieva slips away. At the dance, people sweat, the fiddle wails, and Ieva attracts approving attention. While her mother sings hymns in another room, the young man courts her. After the dance, the pair enjoy some private intimacy. When they return, Ieva’s mother is furious and Ieva cries. The narrator boasts that they will dance again, tells the mother to stay out of the way, and declares that he will not give Ieva up.

The tone is exuberant, teasing, bodily, and more unruly than a summary such as “a girl goes dancing” suggests. The song pits youth, desire, and public festivity against parental and religious supervision. It also gives the young man an edge of comic aggression: his speech to the mother includes a threat. The piece is not a solemn folk romance. It is a swaggering comic monologue built to produce delight, complicity, and perhaps a little scandal.

The language is an eastern Savonian dialect associated with North Karelia, not “ancient Finnish.” Modern Finnish listeners may find it difficult because it is regional, compressed, and playfully spelled, but it is not a surviving medieval language. *Ieva* instead of *Eeva*, *polokka* instead of *polkka*, and forms such as *nuapurista* instead of standard *naapurista* are part of the sound and character of the joke.

Each verse ends in a rapid refrain made largely of playful syllables. Loituma member Hanni Autere notes in the group’s [verse-by-verse explanation](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/) that almost none of the refrain forms Finnish words; the final *hilijalleen* can be understood as “gradually” or “slowly.” Long before the internet detached sound from meaning, Kettunen had already built a semantically loose, irresistibly repeatable element into the song.

## 4. The words and tune do not meet immediately, 1937–1950

The first recording exposes a complication often erased from short histories.

In 1937 Matti Jurva and the Ramblers orchestra made the first known commercial recording of Kettunen’s *Ievan polokka*. They used Kettunen’s text—but, as [Loituma’s historical note](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/) stresses, they sang it to a completely different melody.

This is not a minor curiosity. It means that “the song” still had at least two separable identities: Kettunen’s printable lyric and a traditional tune circulating under local dance titles. Their now-familiar union had not yet become inevitable.

The path toward the canonical pairing can be seen in three postwar records:

- In 1947 accordionist **Onni Laihanen** recorded an instrumental titled *Ievan polokka*. The [National Library catalogue](https://www.finna.fi/Record/fikka.4603891) identifies it as *Savitaipaleen polkka* and credits the melody as traditional.
- On 17 April 1950 **Jorma Ikävalko and Kulkurit** recorded Kettunen’s words to the traditional melody in a Harry Bergström arrangement. The Finnish national filmography identifies the tune under four overlapping names: *Savitaipaleen polkka*, *Ievan polkka*, *Ievan polokka*, and *Olkkolan piikojen polkka*.
- The 1952 musical comedy *On lautalla pienoinen kahvila* used the song on screen. Its [official film record](https://www.finna.fi/Record/kavi.elonet_elokuva_115752) describes workers dancing and playing pots, lids, and barrels while the polka is performed. The scene embedded the song in postwar popular entertainment rather than presenting it as a museum piece.

A 1953 recording by Kalevi Hartti and Nummi Kvartetti is catalogued as a “North Karelian folk polka,” again with Kettunen credited for the words. The [National Library’s grouped record](https://kansalliskirjasto.finna.fi/Record/fikka.5069172) now links dozens of versions, evidence of how extensively the song moved through Finnish recorded culture.

By the 1950s, then, the modern object was recognizably in place: Kettunen’s dialect narrative, the southeastern traditional melody, and a performance style at home among fiddles, accordions, quartets, dances, film comedy, and communal noise. Later recordings by Arttu Suuntala, Pauli Räsänen, and others kept it in circulation. A 1979 recording under the title *Savitaipaleen polkka* demonstrates that the local instrumental identity never vanished completely beneath Ieva’s story.

The song nevertheless receded from the Finnish mainstream during the later twentieth century. It survived as repertoire—particularly among folk musicians and accordionists—without functioning as a current national hit. That distinction made its next transformation possible: trained revival musicians could encounter it as material that was familiar, neglected, and available for radical rearrangement.

## 5. Loituma rebuilds the song with voices, 1989–1998

Loituma began at the Folk Music Department of Helsinki’s Sibelius Academy in 1989. The original seven-student ensemble was called **Jäykkä Leipä**, or “Stiff Bread.” In 1992 it settled into the quartet of Anita Lehtola-Tollin, Sari Kauranen, Hanni Autere, and Timo Väänänen. The musicians played kanteles and violin as well as singing. Seeking a name that travelled more easily outside Finland, they took *Loituma* from a lake on a map. [The group’s official history](https://www.loituma.fi/en/loituma-2/) describes a repertoire that combined folk songs, older popular music, and original work.

Their *Ievan Polkka* arrangement was first performed on **27 May 1992** at Anita Lehtola-Tollin’s graduation concert. The occasion does not make it Anita’s solo arrangement: Loituma credits the arrangement and new lyric material jointly to **Hanni Autere, Sari Kauranen, Anita Lehtola-Tollin, and Timo Väänänen**. Remarkably, it was nearly left off the quartet’s first album, *Loituma*, in 1995. It survived the selection and was recorded. A widely circulated performance on Yle TV2’s *Soi soitto soi* followed in 1996. The band was named Ensemble of the Year at the Kaustinen Folk Music Festival in 1997, and the debut album was issued in the United States in 1998 under the title *Things of Beauty*. Loituma released a second album and toured in Europe and the United States; its main active period lasted until 2008. These dates come from the group’s [official song history](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/) and [career chronology](https://www.loituma.fi/en/career/).

### What Loituma changed

Loituma did not merely perform the familiar song without instruments. Nor did it simply “strip away the traditional accordion”: there was no single canonical accordion arrangement from which every later version descended. The quartet redistributed the entire musical mechanism across four bodies.

Voices supply drone, bass, rhythm, harmony, percussion, and melody. Fragments of Kettunen’s words become accompaniment. The tight texture makes the piece sound at once old and startlingly modern: a dance band reconstructed as vocal interlock.

In the middle, Hanni Autere takes an extended solo made from spontaneously chosen syllables. According to [Loituma’s own account](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/), the solo has no hidden translation. It is improvised vocal play. Its syllables vary among performances. This is distinct from Kettunen’s recurring nonsense refrain: Loituma enlarged a pre-existing principle of playful sound into a new, open solo section.

The distinction is crucial for understanding everything that followed. The part most of the world would come to identify as *Ievan Polkka* was neither the old folk melody alone nor Kettunen’s 1928 lyric. It was new material created inside Loituma’s copyrighted arrangement and performance.

## 6. A twenty-seven-second fragment escapes, 2006

In April 2006, someone joined approximately twenty-seven seconds of Autere’s improvised solo to four animation frames showing **Orihime Inoue**, a character from the anime *Bleach*, twirling a long green onion. The images came from the second episode, in a scene related to Orihime’s alarming culinary imagination. The cartoon vegetable is normally identified in Japanese as a *negi*—a green or Japanese bunching onion—rather than a European leek. The botanically loose English name **Leekspin** nevertheless prevailed.

The origin is not perfectly documented. Meme histories commonly credit the Russian LiveJournal user **g_r_e_e_n**, who linked the animation on 23 April 2006, but references to the loop have been reported from 20 April. It is therefore better to describe g_r_e_e_n as the earliest widely credited distributor or possible creator, not as an author proven beyond doubt. What can be dated more securely is its outward spread: the single-purpose **Leekspin.com** site launched on 6 May. The surviving chronology is summarized by [Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/leekspin-loituma-girl).

The loop also became known as **Loituma Girl**, even though it depicted Orihime and none of the Loituma musicians. Dedicated single-purpose sites let the animation run indefinitely and sometimes counted how long a visitor had watched. Copies travelled through LiveJournal, image boards, forums, Flash sites, YouTube, and ringtone services.

The construction was exceptionally portable:

- four frames required almost no bandwidth;
- the audio returned to its beginning without demanding resolution;
- the singer’s syllables did not require translation;
- the rhythm synchronized with the repeated arm movement;
- neither component explained the other, making the result strange without making it difficult.

This was not simply a recording becoming popular. The meme selected one new part of one arrangement and installed it as a self-contained work. Millions of listeners first encountered the song without Ieva, the mother, the dance, Kettunen, Finland, or even Loituma’s full performance.

The fragment nevertheless led people back upstream. Loituma received international attention and fan mail, performed in the United States in 2006, appeared on European television, and released an *Ieva’s Polka* EP through Universal Music Germany in 2007. The band’s dormant track had become an accidental international calling card eleven years after it was recorded.

Commercial imitation arrived immediately. The 2006 **Holly Dolly** version turned the tune into a sped-up novelty dance track fronted by an animated donkey. Advertisers and ringtone sellers treated the melody, the loop, and the vegetable imagery as interchangeable units. This period fixed several of the song’s new names—“Leekspin Song,” “Loituma’s Polka,” and the typographic error “Levan Polkka”—in international search culture.

## 7. Hatsune Miku inherits the leek, 2007

Crypton Future Media released the Hatsune Miku singing voice software on 31 August 2007. Four days later, on **4 September 2007** in Japan, the producer **Otomania** uploaded a Miku performance of *Ievan Polkka* to the Japanese video site Nico Nico Douga. The surviving [upload metadata](https://www.nicochart.jp/watch/sm982882) identifies video **sm982882**, posted at 12:55:53, with a running time of 2:26. Sources that give 3 September are usually converting the Japanese timestamp to another time zone.

The accompanying animation depicted a tiny, deformed version of Miku waving a *negi* in direct homage to Loituma Girl. Otomania made the music and video; illustrator **Yukino Tamago** created the visual form of the character. Crypton’s [official Miku blog](https://blog.piapro.net/2009/09/post-280.html) later thanked both as the people who brought **Hachune Miku** into being. The green onion became one of Hatsune Miku’s most durable fan-derived attributes. It later appeared in licensed merchandise and official projects, but calling it Miku’s permanent “official signature item” reverses the sequence: the association was created by fans and subsequently adopted by the rights holder. The object did not migrate from Finnish folklore directly into Japanese pop culture. It travelled by way of *Bleach*, Russian LiveJournal, Flash culture, and a Western mistranslation of a Japanese vegetable.

The timing made the video consequential beyond this particular song. Miku had existed as a commercial product for only days. *Ievan Polkka* became one of the earliest viral demonstrations that users could make the new voice perform, draw a character for it, animate that character, publish the result, and inspire further derivatives. It was not, however, “the first mainstream smash hit for Vocaloid software.” Yamaha’s [official Vocaloid history](https://www.vocaloid.com/en/anniversary/history) begins in 2004, and the Japanese voice libraries Meiko and Kaito predated Miku. Miku’s own emergence also involved several early user-made works rather than a single authorized debut hit. Crypton later emphasized that collaborative user production was central to Miku’s cultural life; its [official profile](https://www.crypton.co.jp/miku_eng) describes Miku as software and a licensed character rather than a conventional human recording artist.

The Miku adaptation therefore represents a second major change in the song’s mode of existence. Leekspin had separated a recorded fragment from its performers. The Vocaloid version separated the vocal line from a human singer altogether and made the song a test pattern for participatory synthetic media. It later appeared in the *Hatsune Miku: Project DIVA* rhythm-game series, often as tutorial music, so that a Finnish comic dance song also became a lesson in how to operate a Japanese game about a virtual singer.

[Yle’s 2010 report from Savitaipale](https://yle.fi/a/3-5671487) found the municipality’s cultural secretary amused by the Japanese revival and noted that the tune remained standard local accordion and market-square repertoire. That juxtaposition is the entire history in miniature: a familiar request for an older musician in South Karelia and a virtual global pop performance were now two contemporaneous lives of the same melody.

## 8. From cover song to global template, 2008–2019

After Leekspin and Miku, *Ievan Polkka* no longer belonged to a single genre or audience. New versions did not need to know Kettunen’s whole story; they could inherit whichever component their makers recognized.

Finnish and international musicians returned it to acoustic folk, choral singing, dance music, electronic music, metal, and instrumental virtuosity. The Finnish folk-metal band **Korpiklaani** recorded it for *Manala* in 2012. The vocal group **Tuuletar** made a new Finnish a cappella version in 2018, effectively answering Loituma in the medium Loituma had established. Classical novelty ensembles, DJs, accordionists, flautists, metal bands, and school choirs found different things to preserve.

The song also entered advertising, television, and games. These appearances reinforced a compressed international meaning—fast, cheerful, Finnish, slightly absurd—even when neither the dialect narrative nor the history was present.

This phase can look like mere repetition because so many versions use the same melodic hook. In fact, it established *Ievan Polkka* as a **template**: a work recognizable after drastic changes in language, instrumentation, singer, tempo, image, and setting. Very few songs can lose their story, their original voice, and sometimes nearly all their words while remaining unmistakably themselves.

## 9. The vibing cat, 2019–2021

The song’s second great meme cycle began with a human performance rather than an animation.

Turkish musician **Bilal Göregen**, singing while playing a darbuka, performed the familiar nonsensical section on a park bench. A copy circulated on YouTube in 2019. In October 2020, an editor placed **CatJAM**, a white cat rhythmically nodding its head, beside Göregen. The combination spread through Twitter, Reddit, Instagram, Twitch, and YouTube. Göregen embraced the meme and uploaded an official cat version in November.

A contemporary [Hürriyet Daily News account](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/visually-impaired-turkish-musician-in-cat-vibing-to-ievan-polka-video-wants-to-play-for-us-president-elect-160690) describes the 2020 explosion; an [interview with Göregen](https://knowyourmeme.com/editorials/interviews/bilal-goregen-from-the-cat-vibing-to-street-musicians-ievan-polkka-meme-fills-us-in-on-what-its-like-to-become-an-internet-celebrity-overnight) records his encounter with sudden international recognition. In 2021 South African musician **The Kiffness** built a live-looping remix around Göregen’s performance.

The result was a compact international collaboration assembled without a central producer: a Finnish-Russian melodic lineage, Finnish dialect song, Finnish a cappella improvisation, Turkish voice and drum, a Canadian cat clip, a South African remix, and a worldwide audience. The vegetable disappeared, but the method established by Leekspin remained: isolate a rhythmically perfect fragment, pair it with a tiny repeated gesture, and let recognition do the rest.

## 10. Return to Finnish public culture

Internet fame did not freeze Loituma’s version in 1995. The musicians have periodically reunited and have published later performances, including a 2019 concert video, a remotely assembled 2021 “covid version,” and a new filmed interpretation in 2024. Their [official site](https://www.loituma.fi/en/ievan-polkka-en/) now supplies historical notes, a narrative translation, tutorials, credits, and copyright guidance—an effort to reconnect a radically distributed fragment with the people and story behind it.

At the Eurovision Song Contest in Malmö in 2024, *Ievan Polkka* appeared in a comic Finland sequence involving folk-costume imagery, sauna towels, and Moomin characters before Käärijä’s *Cha Cha Cha*. By then the tune could function as instant international shorthand for Finland. That status had not been conferred by a government, a national broadcaster, a record label, or a planned cultural campaign. It emerged from the accumulated recognitions of local musicians, record buyers, folk revivalists, anime fans, software users, meme makers, and cover artists.

## 11. What remains traditional—and what does not

Calling *Ievan Polkka* “traditional” without qualification obscures its contributors. Calling it an authored pop song obscures the commons from which it grew. The clearest account separates its layers:

| Layer | Principal source or contributor | Historical status |
|---|---|---|
| Melodic family | Oral tradition in southeastern Finland and neighbouring western Russia | Traditional; exact origin uncertain |
| Polka form and local dance use | Nineteenth-century European dance circulation and Finnish regional musicians | Traditional adaptation |
| Narrative lyrics | Eino Kettunen, published 1928 | Authored work |
| Familiar recorded words-and-tune pairing | Onni Laihanen’s 1947 instrumental lineage; Jorma Ikävalko’s 1950 vocal recording | Recorded consolidation |
| Four-voice arrangement and new lyric parts | Hanni Autere, Sari Kauranen, Anita Lehtola-Tollin, and Timo Väänänen / Loituma | Collectively credited arrangement, first performed 1992 and recorded 1995 |
| Extended improvised solo | Hanni Autere within Loituma’s arrangement | Variable performance material |
| Leekspin / Loituma Girl | Uncertain maker; widely associated with Russian LiveJournal circulation in April 2006 | Vernacular internet remix |
| Hatsune Miku version | Otomania and associated fan creators, September 2007 | User-produced synthetic-media adaptation |
| Vibing Cat version | Bilal Göregen’s performance plus later meme editors, 2019–2020 | Distributed performance remix |

These layers also carry different legal rights. A traditional melody, Kettunen’s lyrics, Loituma’s arrangement, particular sound recordings, anime frames, and later videos are not one copyright object. In Finland, the general term for an identified author’s work is seventy years from the end of the author’s death year under [section 43 of the Copyright Act](https://www.finlex.fi/api/media/statute-foreign-language-translation/689238/mainPdf/main.pdf). Because Kettunen died in 1964, his lyrics remain protected there through the end of 2034, even though the underlying traditional tune is not his. Terms and exceptions differ by country. The point is historical rather than legalistic: “folk song” never meant that every layer was anonymous or free of obligations.

## 12. Why this particular song keeps surviving

The song’s longevity is not explained by catchiness alone. Its form gives later participants unusually many places to enter.

### It can lose words without losing identity

The melody is strong enough to survive as an instrumental, while the nonsense refrains and improvised syllables let a singer perform intensity without communicating a proposition. This makes the song unusually easy to cross language boundaries.

### It can be compressed

A few seconds contain the rhythm, melodic contour, and vocal character of the whole. That made it ideal for ringtones, loops, reaction clips, tutorials, and short-form video long before contemporary short-video platforms existed.

### It invites embodiment

The original story is about feet demanding to dance. Later versions keep finding a body: dancers, Loituma’s articulating mouths, Orihime’s arm, Miku’s green onion, Göregen’s hands, a cat’s head. The visual history is not arbitrary decoration. Every successful image discovers another small movement that the polka can animate.

### It tolerates misunderstanding

International listeners misread the title, mistook a bunching onion for a leek, treated improvisation as Finnish lyrics, attributed the song to Loituma or Miku, and often never learned that Ieva existed. Those errors did not stop transmission. They became handles by which new communities carried the piece. Better history does not require pretending the mistakes were accurate; it shows how even inaccurate recognition can keep a cultural work alive.

### It has no final version

The 1937 recording used the words with the “wrong” melody. The 1947 record used the melody without the words. The 1950 record joined them. Loituma removed the instruments and added new syllables. Leekspin removed almost the entire song. Miku replaced the singer. Göregen replaced the vocal ensemble with voice and drum. Each supposed reduction created another branch.

## 14. Common myths corrected

**“It is called *Levan Polkka*.”**  
No. That is a lowercase-l-for-capital-I error. The name is *Ievan Polkka*.

**“Loituma wrote the song.”**  
Loituma created the arrangement and additional vocal material that became globally famous. Kettunen wrote the narrative lyrics in 1928; the melody is traditional.

**“The viral section is ancient Finnish.”**  
No. Hanni Autere’s long solo consists of improvised syllables without a fixed semantic meaning.

**“Anita arranged Loituma’s version by herself.”**  
No. The premiere occurred at Anita Lehtola-Tollin’s graduation concert, but Loituma credits the arrangement and new lyric parts to all four members. “Anita Lehtomäki,” a name found in some summaries, is also not the name given in the group’s official record.

**“Hatsune Miku made it famous first.”**  
No. Miku’s September 2007 version inherited the green-onion imagery from the 2006 Loituma Girl meme, which used Loituma’s 1995 recording.

**“The green onion is Miku’s original official signature item.”**  
No. Otomania and Yukino Tamago created Hachune Miku as a fan response to Leekspin. Crypton later recognized and licensed the association, but it was not part of Miku’s original character design.

**“It was the first Vocaloid hit.”**  
No. It was one of the earliest viral Hatsune Miku demonstrations. Vocaloid products, recordings, and an online user culture already existed before Miku’s release.

**“The anime girl is Hatsune Miku.”**  
The original Leekspin character is Orihime Inoue from *Bleach*. Hachune Miku is a later homage.

**“She is spinning a leek.”**  
The anime object is more accurately a Japanese bunching onion or *negi*. “Leek” is the internationally successful mistake.

**“It is an eighteenth-century polka.”**  
The melodic lineage may reach that far, but the polka genre is nineteenth-century. The familiar tune is best understood as an older melodic inheritance reshaped by later dance practice.

**“Kettunen’s words are already public domain in Finland.”**  
Not yet. Kettunen died in 1964, and Finland’s seventy-year post-mortem term protects the lyrics through 31 December 2034. The traditional melody and later arrangements or recordings are separate rights layers.

**“It is *Säkkijärven polkka*.”**  
No. That is another famous Finnish/Karelian polka with a separate history.

## Conclusion: a song made of handoffs

The most remarkable fact about *Ievan Polkka* is not that an old song survived. Many old songs survive. It is that different communities kept preserving different properties of it.

Savitaipale musicians preserved a dance tune. Kettunen gave it characters, conflict, dialect, and comic desire. Record makers stabilized a words-and-melody pairing. Loituma preserved the propulsion while rebuilding the instrumentation out of voices. A meme maker recognized that twenty-seven seconds could live alone. Otomania recognized it as a vehicle for a new kind of singer. Göregen recognized that it could be carried by one voice and one drum. Millions of listeners recognized that a leek, a green onion, a virtual girl, or a nodding cat could dance to it.

No one planned the complete object. No single contributor possessed the whole future song. Its continuity arose from a chain of partial recognitions: this melody is worth playing; these words are worth printing; this neglected song is worth arranging; this solo is worth looping; this loop is worth answering.

That is the complete history of *Ievan Polkka* as far as the surviving record permits: not the biography of a fixed composition, but the institutional life of a cultural form that repeatedly found new stewards.
