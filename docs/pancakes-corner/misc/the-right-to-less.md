# The Right to Less

## Instagram, Exit Friction, and Institutional Capture

A small software annoyance can reveal a surprisingly large institutional problem.

On 29 October 2026, the author used Instagram in Firefox on a laptop and opened **Content preferences** in the web interface. The page showed one setting, **Sensitive content**. It did not show **Reset suggested content**. Two screenshots retained in the maintainer work area record the complete **Content preferences** page visible in that session and the **Sensitive content control** opened from it. This is a direct observation of that account's web interface on that date.

This is not an exotic request.

The person is not asking Instagram to disappear. They are not necessarily deleting their account. They are not demanding that the service fundamentally change its business model.

They are asking for less.

Less algorithmic influence.

Less accumulated inference.

Less continuity between past behaviour and future recommendations.

Less Instagram in their life.

In this observed case, the reset could not be completed through the web interface. [Meta's published reset instructions](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/11/introducing-recommendations-reset-instagram/) describe tapping through a profile menu and **Content preferences**, but do not say that the steps are for the mobile app or explain what a browser user should do. In this case, that omission concealed a practical requirement from the person looking for the control: to follow the published steps, they would have to move to the app. The documentation gap adds a second obstacle to the missing web control.

For this browser-only user, the documented route to the reset required installing more Instagram in order to ask for less Instagram.

The reset itself has a narrower effect than stopping recommendation profiling. Meta says recommendations begin to personalize again as the person interacts with content. Resetting suggested content, limiting future data use, and turning recommendations off are different requests.

That is not merely poor interface design.

It is an institutional design failure.

---

# 1. The Institutional Relationship

Pancakes treats participation as more than a binary question of membership.

A person can:

- participate;
- dissent;
- partially withdraw;
- revoke a permission;
- reduce a relationship;
- change how they participate;
- retain some rights while ending others;
- or exit entirely.

These distinctions matter because most relationships with institutions are not all-or-nothing.

A person might want to continue using an account while disabling one kind of personalization.

They might want messages but not recommendations.

They might want an archive but not a feed.

They might want the web interface but not an installed application.

They might want to retain social relationships while reducing the platform's role in mediating them.

A humane institution should make these intermediate states possible.

Pancakes calls this **partial withdrawal**.

Withdrawal reduces or ends a particular form of participation, while exit ends or relocates the institutional relationship to the greatest extent available. A person might revoke one data permission while remaining a member, leave one governance layer while remaining affected by another, or stop contributing without surrendering accrued rights.

All-or-nothing exit makes ordinary boundary-setting unnecessarily destructive.

The ability to say **less** is therefore an important institutional capability.

---

# 2. The Asymmetry of Entry and Exit

Digital platforms are usually excellent at increasing participation.

Installing an application is easy.

Enabling notifications is easy.

Following another account is easy.

Allowing contacts access is easy.

Watching another video is easy.

Accepting another recommendation is effortless.

The architecture is optimized for continuation.

Reduction is different.

Settings may be buried.

Controls may differ between mobile and web clients.

Data export may take several steps.

Deletion may involve waiting periods.

Recommendation controls may exist only in a particular interface.

The relevant question is not whether a user is technically capable of reducing their participation.

The better question is:

> How much institutional friction appears when the user attempts to reduce the institution's role?

This is an **exit asymmetry**.

The institution makes increasing participation easier than decreasing it.

That asymmetry becomes especially significant when the institution benefits economically from continued attention, behavioural data, installed applications, notifications, or repeated engagement.

The problem does not require malicious intent.

It can emerge naturally from organizational incentives.

Teams are rewarded for adoption.

Product roadmaps prioritize active clients.

Analytics focus on retention.

Mobile applications receive more development effort.

Features associated with engagement receive stronger internal sponsorship than features associated with disengagement.

The result can still be institutionally significant even when nobody explicitly decided to make withdrawal difficult.

Institutions are shaped by incentives as well as intentions.

# 3. Jurisdictional Expansion

There is another boundary hidden inside the Instagram example.

A website and an installed mobile application do not occupy the same relationship with a person.

A browser session is comparatively bounded.

An installed application may potentially participate in a much wider technical environment:

- notifications;
- background activity;
- device permissions;
- operating-system integrations;
- persistent credentials;
- deep links;
- contact access;
- photo access;
- camera and microphone access;
- app-specific telemetry;
- and more durable behavioural presence.

Not every application uses every capability, and operating systems impose their own permission controls.

But the institutional relationship is still different.

The person approached Instagram through one boundary:

```text
person
    ↓
web browser
    ↓
Instagram
```

The institution answers:

```text
To change this aspect of our relationship,
install our software more deeply into your personal device.
```

If installation is the only available path to the control, that requirement creates a form of **jurisdictional expansion**.

The design moves the relationship into a broader technical jurisdiction as a condition of exercising a narrower form of user control. The observed interface does not establish why Instagram made that design choice.

The request was:

```text
reduce recommendation influence
```

The required transition becomes:

```text
browser relationship
    ↓
mobile application installation
    ↓
recommendation control
```

The remedy requires an expansion of the institutional footprint.

That inversion matters.

# 4. Practical Freedom and Nominal Freedom

Institutions often satisfy themselves with formal rights.

The option exists.

The checkbox exists.

The support page exists.

The account can theoretically be deleted.

The data can theoretically be exported.

But Pancakes distinguishes **formal exit** from **usable freedom**.

A right that carries unreasonable transition costs may be legally or technically available while remaining practically weak.

Exit should therefore be understood as a path:

```text
desire to reduce participation
    ↓
locate the relevant control
    ↓
understand its consequences
    ↓
exercise the control
    ↓
retain appropriate rights and records
    ↓
continue, withdraw, or leave
```

Every unnecessary obstacle along that path changes the effective character of the right.

If a user must install software they deliberately chose not to install merely to change recommendation behaviour, then the institution has imposed a transition cost on boundary-setting.

The control exists.

But the user's freedom is less usable than it appears.

This distinction is essential in digital systems.

Software can comply with a narrow interpretation of user choice while still making meaningful autonomy difficult.

---

# 5. Participation Does Not Mean Endorsement

Platform institutions routinely observe behaviour.

They know that someone:

- opened the service;
- clicked;
- watched;
- scrolled;
- returned;
- remained subscribed;
- installed an application;
- or continued maintaining an account.

These behaviours are useful operational signals.

They are not reliable evidence of legitimacy.

Pancakes describes the **participation fallacy**:

```text
the person uses the service
therefore
the person accepts the arrangement
```

The inference does not follow.

People continue participating for many reasons.

They may value their relationships while disliking the platform.

They may rely on a service because everyone they know uses it.

They may tolerate one harmful characteristic because another function remains useful.

They may lack a credible substitute.

They may be gradually reducing their involvement.

They may simply not have completed the work required to leave.

Observed participation is evidence that a relationship exists.

It is not proof that the participant endorses every term of that relationship.

A user asking Instagram for fewer recommendations is therefore not contradicting themselves by continuing to have an Instagram account.

They are exercising governance within an ongoing relationship.

That should be recognized as legitimate participation.

---

# 6. The Institutional Meaning of “Less”

Modern software tends to treat user preferences as optimization parameters.

Show more of this.

Show less of that.

Tune the ranking function.

Adjust the notifications.

Change the theme.

But some preferences are not merely personalization.

They are **claims about institutional jurisdiction**.

Consider the difference:

```text
I prefer landscape photos.
```

and:

```text
I want this system to exert less influence over my attention.
```

The first is a content preference.

The second is a boundary.

Similarly:

```text
Do not recommend cooking videos.
```

is different from:

```text
Stop constructing recommendations from my accumulated behavioural history.
```

The latter concerns the relationship between the institution and the person.

It is closer to revocation than personalization.

This suggests an important design distinction.

Platforms should separate:

```text
preference controls
```

from:

```text
relationship controls
```

Preference controls tune the institution.

Relationship controls constrain the institution.

The second category deserves stronger protection.

---

# 7. Capture

The pattern raises the question of **capture**. A single missing web control is a warning sign, not by itself proof that the institution as a whole has been captured.

Capture occurs when an institution stops merely serving a participant and begins organizing the participant's choices around the institution's own continuity.

A healthy institution develops the capability of the people who depend on it.

An extractive institution develops dependence.

The difference can sometimes be seen in very small design decisions.

A healthy system might say:

```text
You want less of this relationship.

Here are the controls.

They work wherever you currently access the service.
```

A captured system tends toward:

```text
You want less of this relationship.

First enter further into our preferred environment.
```

This is why the Instagram example feels disproportionately irritating.

The request and the remedy point in opposite directions.

The user says:

```text
less Instagram
```

The system says:

```text
install Instagram
```

The semantic contradiction is immediate.

The institution cannot comfortably represent reduction except through greater participation.

That is a warning sign.

---

# 8. A Constitutional Principle for Software

Pancakes treats exit as a constitutional safeguard against domination.

That principle should apply directly to software.

A useful design rule is:

> **Without a proportionate security, legal, or operational reason, the cost of reducing an institution's authority over a person should not increase with the degree to which the person wishes to reduce that authority.**

The principle has several practical consequences.

Controls for the following should be broadly available:

- reducing personalization;
- disabling recommendation history;
- revoking permissions;
- disabling notifications;
- limiting data use;
- exporting records;
- suspending participation;
- deleting data;
- deleting an account;
- migrating elsewhere.

And these controls should not require:

- installation of additional software;
- acceptance of new permissions;
- participation in a more invasive client;
- identity verification beyond what is needed to protect the account and affected people;
- surrender of unrelated rights;
- or engagement with additional institutional surfaces.

The strongest autonomy controls should exist at the **boundary** of the institution.

That is precisely where people need them.

---

# 9. Designing for Reduction

Most software product design asks:

```text
How do we help people do more?
```

Institutional engineering must also ask:

```text
How do we help people safely do less?
```

That question produces different requirements.

A system designed for reduction would support:

### Partial withdrawal

Users can stop one form of participation without destroying the entire relationship.

### Client parity for governance

Important account, privacy, recommendation, export, and exit controls remain available across reasonable client surfaces.

### No retaliatory friction

Reducing engagement does not create unnecessary inconvenience elsewhere.

### Explicit relationship states

The system can represent:

```text
active
reduced
paused
read-only
non-personalized
locally retained
exported
departed
```

rather than only:

```text
user
not user
```

### Reversible boundaries

Where appropriate, participants can change their minds without rebuilding the relationship from scratch.

### Institutional humility

The service recognizes that successful use does not necessarily mean the person wants more of it.

# 10. Metrics Can Hide the Failure

There is a final difficulty.

Many ordinary product metrics would interpret the Instagram sequence positively.

Suppose someone:

1. visits Instagram on the web;
2. discovers that recommendation reset requires the application;
3. installs the application;
4. signs in;
5. navigates through settings;
6. resets the recommendation system.

A conventional analytics system might record:

```text
web user
→ mobile conversion
→ successful authentication
→ settings engagement
→ retained account
```

Several dashboards might turn green.

Institutionally, however, something very different happened.

The person was trying to reduce the institution's influence.

The institution converted that attempt into deeper technical adoption.

This demonstrates why behavioural metrics cannot settle institutional meaning.

The same event can simultaneously be:

```text
Product analytics:
successful app conversion

User experience:
annoying workaround

Governance:
constrained partial withdrawal

Institutional analysis:
jurisdictional expansion

Business metric:
increased mobile adoption
```

The event is the same.

The interpretation depends on what the observer is trying to measure.

Institutions that only measure growth will systematically fail to perceive many forms of domination.

---

# 11. The Right to Less

Digital rights are often described in dramatic terms:

- privacy;
- deletion;
- portability;
- security;
- freedom of expression.

Those rights matter.

But everyday institutional autonomy also depends on a quieter capability:

> **the right to have less of the institution.**

Less personalization.

Less monitoring.

Less recommendation.

Less communication.

Less persistence.

Less integration.

Less dependency.

Less attention.

Less relationship.

A person should not have to completely abandon an institution merely to establish a boundary within it.

And they certainly should not have to deepen the relationship first.

The Instagram settings problem is therefore useful precisely because it is so mundane.

Nothing catastrophic has happened.

The user is simply irritated.

But irritation can be institutional information.

The person has encountered a boundary and discovered that the system recognizes expansion more easily than contraction.

That is a small example of a larger design question:

```text
Does the institution make people
more capable of governing their relationship with it?

or

does it make the institution
more capable of governing them?
```

The difference between those two architectures is the difference between stewardship and capture.

And sometimes you can see the whole distinction in a missing settings button.
